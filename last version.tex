\title{Marginal Contrastive Discrimination for Interventional Density Estimation\\
Under a Continuous, Multidimensional Treatment}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We study the estimation of the interventional density of a scalar outcome under a
continuous, possibly multidimensional treatment. Under the back-door adjustment, this
target reduces to a single conditional density whose conditioning set contains the
treatment and every confounder, and which is therefore high-dimensional while the outcome
is scalar. Marginal contrastive discrimination (MCD) is tailored to this asymmetry: it
replaces the conditional density by a univariate marginal and a binary classifier, so that no
density on a high-dimensional space is ever fitted. The resulting plug-in estimator,
however, inherits a first-order bias. Removing that bias by the standard one-step
correction requires the influence function (IF) of the target, which contains the generalized
propensity score, itself a conditional density in the treatment, and precisely the
object the contrastive construction was meant to avoid. Our main contribution is to
re-express the same correction through the contrastive factorization. The generalized
propensity score then disappears from the formula; in exchange, the correction now requires
the influence function of the fitted contrast function, whose training sample is built from
the data and which therefore behaves unlike the influence function of an ordinarily trained
model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A prediction describes what is likely to happen; a causal statement describes what would
happen under an intervention. Decisions call for the second. A clinician choosing a dose, a
regulator setting an exposure limit, and an analyst evaluating a policy all need the
distribution of an outcome under an intervention. Randomized experiments answer such
questions by construction, but they are often unavailable, and the causal quantity must then
be recovered from observational data, in which the treatment is chosen rather than assigned
and the reasons for the choice usually affect the outcome as well.

Most of the causal-inference literature reports a mean: the average treatment effect or, for
a treatment ranging over a continuum, the dose-response curve $t \mapsto \pE[Y(t)]$, where
$Y(t)$ is the potential outcome under treatment level $t$. A mean, however, collapses a
distribution to a single number, and two interventions with equal means may induce very
different outcome distributions. Whenever the question concerns the probability of exceeding
a threshold, the spread of the response, or any functional of the outcome law beyond its
first moment, the relevant object is the full interventional density
\[
p_t(y) = \pf{Y(t)}(y),
\]
the density of $Y(t)$ at the point $y$. Its semiparametric theory in the discrete-treatment
case is due to \citet{kennedy2023density}, who derived the efficient influence function and a
doubly robust, augmented inverse-probability-of-treatment-weighted (A-IPTW) estimator;
representing its
nuisance by a conditional normalizing flow and its target by a truncated orthogonal series
gives the \textsc{cnf+ts} estimator of the comparison below.
\citet{melnychuk2023flows} instead represent both the nuisance and the target directly as
normalizing flows and A-IPTW-correct the result (\textsc{infs+}), and report two ablations of
their own architecture as further baselines: a covariate-adjusted flow pair without the
correction (\textsc{infs}), and a version with no target flow at all, using the nuisance
flow's conditional density directly as a plug-in, identical, as they note, to the
pre-existing conditional normalizing flow baseline (\textsc{cnf}, \citealp{trippe2018cnf}).
Their comparison also includes two uncorrected regression baselines, a shared neural
representation with a homoscedastic Gaussian output (\textsc{tarnet*},
\citealp{shalit2017tarnet}) or a Gaussian-mixture output (\textsc{mdn},
\citealp{bishop1994mdn}), and two nonparametric kernel baselines, a plug-in kernel mean
embedding (\textsc{dkme}, \citealp{muandet2021counterfactual}) and an A-IPTW kernel estimator
(\textsc{kde}, \citealp{kim2018distributional}). Separately, \citet{ham2024logconcave} impose
log-concavity on the counterfactual density, and \citet{martinez2023stein} avoid an explicit
density representation altogether, via kernel Stein discrepancies. All of the above assume a
treatment with finitely many levels.

This paper treats the continuous case, which is both more common in applications and harder
in theory. Doses, durations, concentrations and continuous policy instruments are not
naturally discretized, and applications often involve several such quantities at once, so
the treatment is a vector $T \in \cT \subseteq \R^d$. With a continuous treatment the
propensity score becomes the \emph{generalized} propensity score of \citet{imbens2000} and
\citet{hirano2004}, a conditional density in $t$ rather than a probability, and the target
is no longer estimable at the parametric rate: \citet{kennedy2017continuous} showed that the
dose-response curve requires smoothing in the treatment argument, and \citet{colangelo2020}
gave the corresponding debiased machine-learning estimator; more recently,
\citet{hudson2023dose} study nonparametric inference for the same dose-response curve under
weaker overlap. In all of this line of work the target has remained the mean. The density
literature runs in the opposite direction: \citet{kennedy2023density} and the baselines listed
above target the full counterfactual density, but only for a treatment with finitely many
levels. To our knowledge no prior work targets the density under a continuous treatment; here
we keep the treatment continuous, and vector-valued, but take the target to be the density.

Our identification rests on the back-door adjustment of \citet{pearl1995,pearl2009}, which
expresses the target as the conditional outcome density averaged over the covariates,
\[
p_t(y) = \pE_X\big[\pf{Y \mid T=t,X}(y)\big].
\]
Estimation therefore reduces to a single conditional density, evaluated at the treatment
level $t$ and averaged over the covariate distribution. This is where the difficulty
concentrates. The conditioning set is the pair $(T,X)$: it holds the treatment and every
confounder, and is high-dimensional, whereas the outcome is scalar. Standard nonparametric
conditional-density estimators are poorly suited to that asymmetry, because their rates
deteriorate with the dimension of the conditioning argument
\citep{stone1980,Tsybakov2009NonparametricEstimation}, and this rate governs the error of
the whole procedure.

Marginal contrastive discrimination (MCD) \citep{meziani2025mcd} is designed for exactly this
asymmetry. It writes the conditional density as a univariate marginal times a density ratio,
and estimates the ratio by a binary classifier that separates genuine observations from
recombined ones, in which the outcome has been resampled from its own marginal. No density on a high-dimensional
space is ever fitted: the marginal is univariate, and the ratio is recovered by
classification, a problem any supervised learner can solve. The same principle underlies
noise-contrastive estimation \citep{gutmann2010nce} and classification-based density-ratio
estimation \citep{sugiyama2012density}, on which the contrastive construction builds.

Plugging this estimate into the back-door average yields an estimator of $p_t(y)$, but, like
every plug-in built on a nonparametric nuisance, it is biased at first order: the bias
equals the covariate average of the conditional-density error and vanishes only as slowly as
that error \citep{bickel1988,robins2008}. The standard remedy is the one-step correction of
semiparametric theory \citep{vanderlaan2006tmle,chernozhukov2018,kennedy2022review}, which
adds the empirical average of the target's influence function (IF) and renders the estimator
first-order insensitive to the nuisance. That correction, however, runs into a problem specific to our target. The influence function
of $p_t(y)$ contains the generalized propensity score: a second nuisance, and a conditional
density in the treatment coordinate, exactly the object the contrastive construction was
adopted to avoid. Written in the usual way, the correction gives back the difficulty the
estimator was meant to remove.

The resolution exploits a basic fact about influence functions. The influence function of a
target is unique, but the target itself admits several equivalent expressions, and
differentiating any of them yields an equal, though differently written, formula for
the same object. We differentiate the target in its \emph{contrastive} form rather than its
back-door form. In that form the generalized propensity score never appears. The price is a
new object, the influence function of the fitted contrast function, which behaves unlike the
influence function of an ordinarily trained model because the contrastive training sample is
itself built from the data.

\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

This section fixes the estimand and the ingredients used to estimate it. We first record the
causal assumptions under which the interventional density is identified from observational
data, then form the plug-in estimator from an arbitrary estimator of the conditional density,
and show that it is biased at first order. That bias is the object the remainder of the paper
is devoted to removing.

\subsection{Notation}
\label{sec:setting-notation}

Let $T \in \cT \subseteq \R^{d}$ be a treatment, $X \in \cX \subseteq \R^{p}$ a vector of
covariates and $Y \in \cY \subseteq \R$ a scalar outcome. We observe $n$ independent copies
of
\[
W = (T,X,Y) \in \cW = \cT \times \cX \times \cY , \qquad W \sim F,
\]
and assume $F$ has a density with respect to Lebesgue measure. Following
\citet{meziani2025mcd}, we write $\pf{T,X,Y}$, $\pf{T,X}$, $\pf{X}$, $\pf{Y}$ for the joint
and marginal densities and $\pf{Y \mid T=t,X=x}$ for the conditional density of the outcome.
For $t \in \cT$, let $Y(t)$ be the potential outcome under the intervention setting the
treatment to $t$.

\subsection{Causal structure and identification}
\label{sec:setting-id}

We assume the causal structure of Figure~\ref{fig:dag}: the covariates $X$ affect both $T$
and $Y$, the treatment $T$ affects $Y$, and no unobserved variable is a common cause of $T$
and $Y$. The only path between $T$ and $Y$ besides the direct one is
$T \leftarrow X \rightarrow Y$, and conditioning on $X$ blocks it. This is the back-door
criterion of \citet{pearl2009}.

        \node[dagnode, right=of X] (T) {$T$};
        \node[dagnode, right=of T] (Y) {$Y$};
        \draw[dagarrow] (X) -- (T);
        \draw[dagarrow] (X) to[out=20,in=160] (Y);
        \draw[dagarrow] (T) -- (Y);
    \end{tikzpicture}
    \caption{Causal structure assumed throughout. The treatment $T$ is continuous and
    possibly multidimensional, the outcome $Y$ is scalar, and the covariates $X$ affect
    both. The only back-door path $T \leftarrow X \rightarrow Y$ is blocked by conditioning
    on $X$.}
    \label{fig:dag}
\end{figure}

To turn this structure into identification of the potential outcomes
$\{Y(t) : t \in \cT\}$, we impose the standard conditions for continuous treatments, due to
\citet{imbens2000} and \citet{hirano2004}.

\begin{assumption}[Identification]
\label{as:identification}
Let $t \in \cT$ be the treatment level of interest.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] \emph{Consistency and no interference.} $Y = Y(T)$, and a unit's potential
outcomes do not depend on the treatment levels of other units.
\item[(ii)] \emph{Weak unconfoundedness.} $Y(t) \perp T \mid X$.
\item[(iii)] \emph{Positivity.} There exists $\gps_{\min} > 0$ such that
$\pf{T \mid X=x}(t) \ge \gps_{\min}$ for every $x$ with $\pf{X}(x) > 0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

The three parts play distinct roles. Consistency ties the potential outcomes to the observed
data, equating $Y$ with the potential outcome at the treatment actually received. Weak
unconfoundedness is the distributional counterpart of the absent common cause of $T$ and $Y$
in Figure~\ref{fig:dag}: given the covariates, the treatment carries no further information
about the potential outcome. Positivity requires the level $t$ to be reachable at every
covariate value; its lower bound $\gps_{\min}$ is what will keep the correction of
Section~\ref{sec:eif} finite. Under these conditions $Y(t)$ is identified, because weak
unconfoundedness makes the conditional density of $Y$ at $T=t$ equal to the density of $Y(t)$
at each covariate value, and averaging over the covariates recovers the interventional law.

\begin{mydef}[Interventional density]
\label{def:target}
For $t \in \cT$ and $y \in \cY$, the interventional density is
\begin{equation}
p_t(y) \;:=\; \pf{Y(t)}(y) \;=\; \pE_X\big[\eta(X)\big]
\;=\; \int_{\cX} \eta(x)\,\pf{X}(x) \dd x ,
\qquad
\eta(x) := \pf{Y \mid T=t,X=x}(y),
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
where $\eta(x)$ is the conditional outcome density at the point $(t,x,y)$; we suppress its
dependence on the fixed $t$ and $y$.
\end{mydef}

Two features of this estimand matter later. First, the covariate is averaged out while $t$ and
$y$ are fixed evaluation points; this asymmetry is what produces the point masses in the
influence function. Second, the only unknown is the conditional density $\eta$, since the
covariate distribution is handled by the empirical distribution of $X_1,\dots,X_n$. Its
conditioning set $(T,X)$ has dimension $d+p$ while the outcome is scalar, and it is this
imbalance that makes standard estimators inaccurate and motivates the estimator used below.

The correction of Section~\ref{sec:eif} will also involve the conditional law of the
treatment, which we introduce here.

\begin{mydef}[Generalized propensity score]
\label{def:gps}
The conditional density of the treatment given the covariates,
\begin{equation}
\gps(t \mid x) \;:=\; \pf{T \mid X=x}(t),
\label{eq:gps}
\end{equation}
is the generalized propensity score of \citet{imbens2000} and \citet{hirano2004}.
\end{mydef}

For a continuous treatment $\gps(t\mid x)$ is a density in $t \in \R^{d}$, not a probability,
and part~(iii) of Assumption~\ref{as:identification} bounds it below. It plays no role in
identification, the estimand~\eqref{eq:target} does not mention it, and its cost as a
nuisance is precisely what the contrastive route of Section~\ref{sec:mcd-correction} will save.

\subsection{The plug-in estimator and its bias}
\label{sec:setting-plugin}

Let $\widehat\eta$ be any estimator of the conditional density $\eta$, the marginal
contrastive discrimination estimator of Section~\ref{sec:mcd-correction} is one choice, but
nothing in this subsection uses it. Averaging
$\widehat\eta$ over the covariates, as Definition~\ref{def:target} prescribes, gives the
plug-in estimator
\begin{equation}
\widehat p^{\,\mathrm{pi}}_t(y)
\;=\; \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \widehat\eta(X_i).
\label{eq:plugin}
\end{equation}
To see why this estimator is biased, suppose $\widehat\eta$ is fitted on a sample independent
of $X_1,\dots,X_n$, and split the error by adding and subtracting the covariate average of the
true $\eta$:
\begin{equation}
\widehat p^{\,\mathrm{pi}}_t(y) - p_t(y)
= \underbrace{\frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \big( \eta(X_i) - p_t(y) \big)}_{\text{sampling error}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \big( \widehat\eta(X_i) - \eta(X_i) \big)}_{\text{nuisance error}} .
\label{eq:plugin-decomp}
\end{equation}
The two pieces behave in opposite ways. The sampling error averages the fixed function
$x\mapsto\eta(x)-p_t(y)$, which has mean zero because $\pE_X[\eta(X)] = p_t(y)$; it is
therefore centred and of order $n^{-1/2}$, the ordinary fluctuation of a sample mean.

The nuisance error behaves differently. Because
$\widehat\eta$ is fitted on a separate sample, we may treat it as fixed and take expectations
over $X_1,\dots,X_n$ alone. Each term then has the same expectation, so
\begin{equation}
\pE\Big[\, \widehat p^{\,\mathrm{pi}}_t(y) - p_t(y) \,\Big|\, \widehat\eta \,\Big]
= \pE_X\big[ \widehat\eta(X) - \eta(X) \big],
\label{eq:plugin-bias}
\end{equation}
the average error of the conditional-density estimate. Nothing forces this quantity to vanish:
if $\widehat\eta$ over- or under-estimates $\eta$ on average, so does the plug-in. The offset
is a property of the back-door average itself, shared by every conditional-density estimator.

The right-hand side of~\eqref{eq:plugin-bias} is what the semiparametric literature calls the
first-order bias of the plug-in, and the name deserves a word of caution, since it is not the
bias of an estimator in the elementary sense. The finite-sample bias
$\pE[\widehat\theta]-\theta$ at a fixed model would vanish as the nuisance became consistent;
the quantity in~\eqref{eq:plugin-bias} is a different object, namely the leading term of the
estimation error expanded in the nuisance error $\widehat\eta-\eta$, and it is of first order
precisely because it is linear in that error. The same term recurs across the literature under
names that stress different facets of it: an obstruction to efficient estimation with an
infinite-dimensional nuisance \citep{bickel1988,robins2008}, a regularization bias inherited
from the smoothing that any flexible estimator applies \citep{chernozhukov2018}, or the plug-in
bias that the one-step correction is built to remove
\citep{vanderlaan2006tmle,kennedy2022review}. We adopt the term first-order bias for the
object~\eqref{eq:plugin-bias}.

A larger sample does not cure this bias. The nuisance error shrinks with $n$, but only at the
rate of $\|\widehat\eta-\eta\|$, and for a conditional density on a conditioning set of
dimension $d+p$ that rate is slower than $n^{-1/2}$ for any flexible estimator: nonparametric
conditional-density estimators have rates that deteriorate with the dimension of the
conditioning argument \citep{stone1980,Tsybakov2009NonparametricEstimation,bashtannyk2001,hall2004},
and with several treatments and confounders this error dominates everything else. On the
$\sqrt n$ scale the first-order bias therefore diverges
rather than settling, so $\sqrt n\big(\widehat p^{\,\mathrm{pi}}_t(y)-p_t(y)\big)$ has no
centred limit and yields no valid confidence interval. The obstruction is not a small sample
but the sub-parametric rate of conditional-density estimation in high dimension, which no
sample size and no choice of estimator overcomes.

For the interventional density the consequence is more damaging than for a mean. The density
is estimated pointwise in $y$, so the first-order bias is not one mislocated number but a
systematic distortion of the whole curve $y\mapsto\widehat p^{\,\mathrm{pi}}_t(y)$. The very quantities that
motivate estimating a density, tail probabilities, dispersion, the chance of exceeding a
threshold, are functionals of this curve, and each inherits the distortion. Correcting the
first-order bias is thus a prerequisite for the inferences the interventional density is meant
to support.

The remedy is to make the nuisance enter only at second order. A second-order term is
quadratic in $\widehat\eta-\eta$, of order $\|\widehat\eta-\eta\|^2$, which is $o(n^{-1/2})$ as
soon as the nuisance converges faster than $n^{-1/4}$, a requirement far weaker than the
parametric rate. The device that achieves this is the one-step correction, to which we now
turn.

\section{First-order correction}
\label{sec:eif}

The plug-in of Section~\ref{sec:setting-plugin} carries the first-order
bias~\eqref{eq:plugin-bias}, and the previous section argued that removing it is necessary
before any inference. The one-step correction does so by adding to the plug-in the empirical
average of an influence function, chosen so that the first-order term cancels and the nuisance
enters only at second order. To build it we need the influence function of $p_t(y)$. We first
recall the von Mises expansion that defines it, then compute it, and then confront the
difficulty it raises: the correction it produces is valid, but it reintroduces the generalized
propensity score. That difficulty is what motivates Section~\ref{sec:mcd-correction}.

\subsection{Von Mises expansion}
\label{sec:eif-vm}

View the target as a functional $\psi(F) = p_t(y)$ of the data law. To probe its sensitivity
to the data, perturb $F$ towards a point mass at $w \in \cW$,
\begin{equation}
F_\varepsilon \;=\; (1-\varepsilon)F + \varepsilon\,\delta_w ,
\qquad \varepsilon \in [0,1),
\label{eq:perturbation}
\end{equation}
and differentiate the target along this path. The derivative is the influence function, in the
sense of \citet{hampel1974} and the semiparametric development of \citet{bickel1993}.

\begin{mydef}[Influence function]
\label{def:if}
The influence function of $\psi$ at $F$ in the direction $\delta_w$ is
\[
\IF(w;\psi,F) \;:=\;
\frac{\partial}{\partial \varepsilon} \psi(F_\varepsilon)\Big|_{\varepsilon=0} .
\]
\end{mydef}

Because $\varepsilon \mapsto \psi(F_\varepsilon)$ is an ordinary real-valued map, its
derivative obeys the ordinary sum, product, quotient and chain rules. We collect these rules,
in the form adapted to $\IF$, in Appendix~\ref{app:toolbox}; they are all the machinery the
computations below require. The influence function has mean zero,
$\pE_F[\IF(W;\psi,F)] = 0$, and it governs the first-order response of the target to a change
of law: for any competing law $F'$,
\begin{equation}
\psi(F') - \psi(F)
\;=\; \pE_{F'}\big[\IF(W;\psi,F)\big] + \Rem_2(F',F),
\label{eq:von-mises}
\end{equation}
with a remainder of second order in $F'-F$. This expansion is what makes bias removal
possible. Reading $F'$ as the true law and $F$ as the estimated one, the plug-in error is the
first-order term plus a second-order remainder; adding the empirical average of the estimated
influence function to the plug-in cancels that first-order term and leaves only the remainder
\citep{bickel1993,vandervaart1998,vanderlaan2006tmle,chernozhukov2018}. It remains to compute
$\IF(w;p_t(y),F)$, which is the task of the next subsection.

\subsection{The influence function of the interventional density}
\label{sec:eif-classical}

We apply Definition~\ref{def:if} to the target. Since $p_t(y)$ is an integral over $x$ of a
ratio of densities in $(t,x,y)$, differentiating it along the path~\eqref{eq:perturbation}
with the calculus of Appendix~\ref{app:toolbox} yields the following; the full derivation is in
Appendix~\ref{app:classical-derivative}.

\begin{mypropo}[Nonparametric correction]
\label{prop:eif}
Under Assumption~\ref{as:identification} and the regularity conditions of
Appendix~\ref{app:classical-regularity}, the influence function of $p_t(y)$ at $F$, evaluated
at $w = (t_w,x_w,y_w)$, is
\begin{equation}
\IF\big(w; p_t(y), F\big)
= \frac{\delta_{t_w}(t)}{\gps(t \mid x_w)}
\Big( \delta_{y_w}(y) - \eta(x_w) \Big)
+ \eta(x_w) - p_t(y),
\label{eq:eif-dirac}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_a$ denotes the Dirac measure at $a$ and $\eta(x_w)=\pf{Y\mid T=t,X=x_w}(y)$.
\end{mypropo}

The shape of~\eqref{eq:eif-dirac} is exactly the shape anticipated after
Definition~\ref{def:target}, and reading it that way explains each term rather than merely
labelling it. Because the covariate is averaged out, a contamination at $x_w$ enters the
target smoothly, and its effect is the ordinary centred comparison $\eta(x_w) - p_t(y)$
between the conditional density at the contaminating covariate and its average, the same
term one would obtain for any smooth average. Because the treatment and outcome are read off
at fixed points, by contrast, a contamination that lands on those points has a singular
effect: it can only register through Dirac masses in $t$ and $y$, which is the first term.
The weight $1/\gps(t\mid x_w)$ on that term is the inverse generalized propensity score, and
it is there for a familiar reason, a contamination at treatment level $t_w$ is informative
about the level $t$ only in proportion to how rarely that level occurs at $x_w$, so its
influence is inflated when $\gps(t\mid x_w)$ is small. The essential observation for what
follows is simply that $\gps$ appears at all: an object absent from the
target~\eqref{eq:target} has entered its influence function.

Two limiting cases place~\eqref{eq:eif-dirac} in the literature and confirm it. For a discrete
treatment the Dirac in $t$ becomes an indicator, the generalized propensity score becomes an
ordinary propensity score, and the expression reduces to the influence function of
\citet{kennedy2023density}. For the mean $\pE[Y(t)]$ the outcome is integrated rather than
evaluated, the Dirac in $y$ disappears, and the expression reduces to that of
\citet{kennedy2017continuous}. Our target combines a continuous treatment with a pointwise
outcome, and both singular features appear together; to our knowledge this influence function
has not been derived before in that combination.

The Dirac masses are not estimable, so we regularize them by kernel smoothing with bandwidths
$h_T$ and $h_Y$; the substitution and its bias are carried out in
Appendix~\ref{app:classical-kernels}. Feeding the smoothed influence function into the
one-step construction of~\eqref{eq:von-mises}, the plug-in~\eqref{eq:plugin} plus the
empirical average of the smoothed influence function, gives the \emph{classical estimator}
below; the plug-in term cancels algebraically against a matching term inside that average
(Appendix~\ref{app:classical-kernels}, display~\eqref{eq:app-onestep}) and so does not appear
in it explicitly.
\begin{equation}
\widehat p^{\,\mathrm{c}}_t(y)
= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\left[ \frac{K_{h_T}(T_i-t)}{\widehat\gps(t \mid X_i)}
\Big( K_{h_Y}(Y_i-y) - \widehat\eta(X_i) \Big)
+ \widehat\eta(X_i) \right].
\label{eq:est-classical}
\end{equation}
Smoothing enters only through the two point evaluations; the nuisances $\gps$ and $\eta$ are
left unsmoothed and may be fitted by any method. This estimator removes the first-order bias
of the plug-in, and does so robustly, in the following precise sense; the remainder is bounded
in Appendix~\ref{app:classical-remainder}.

\begin{mypropo}[Double robustness]
\label{prop:dr-classical}
Under the conditions of Appendix~\ref{app:classical-remainder}, the second-order remainder of
the estimator~\eqref{eq:est-classical} factorizes as a product of the errors of $\gps$ and
$\eta$. Consequently $\widehat p^{\,\mathrm{c}}_t(y)$ is $\sqrt{n}$-consistent whenever each
nuisance converges at rate $n^{-1/4}$, and one nuisance may converge more slowly if the other
converges faster.
\end{mypropo}

This proposition deserves unpacking, because the mechanism behind it is precisely what the
contrastive route of Section~\ref{sec:mcd-correction} gives up, and half of
Section~\ref{sec:discussion-dr} is an accounting of that loss. The name the discrete-treatment
literature gives~\eqref{eq:est-classical}, \emph{augmented} inverse-probability-of-treatment
weighting, describes its construction literally: an inverse-probability weighted estimator to
which one adds an augmentation term built from an outcome model. Three estimators of the same
target $p_t(y) = \pE_X[\eta(X)]$ sit side by side, differing only in which nuisances they use.

The plug-in~\eqref{eq:plugin} averages a fitted outcome model over the covariates and uses no
treatment model at all. The inverse-probability-weighted (IPW) estimator does the opposite, and it
repays deriving rather than quoting, because the change of measure behind it is the same one
that reappears, hidden, inside the augmentation below. Under
Assumption~\ref{as:identification}, weighting the observed law by the inverse generalized
propensity score undoes the dependence of the treatment on the covariates:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\pE\!\left[ \frac{\delta_{T}(t)}{\gps(t \mid X)}\, \delta_{Y}(y) \right]
&= \iiint \pf{T,X,Y}(\tau,x,u)\,
   \frac{\delta_{\tau}(t)}{\gps(t \mid x)}\, \delta_{u}(y) \dd\tau \dd x \dd u \\[2pt]
&= \int \frac{\pf{T,X}(t,x)}{\gps(t \mid x)}\,
   \pf{Y \mid T=t, X=x}(y) \dd x
 \;=\; \int \pf{X}(x)\,\eta(x) \dd x
 \;=\; p_t(y) ,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ipw-identity}
\end{equation}
the last step using the factorization $\pf{T,X}(t,x) = \pf{X}(x)\,\gps(t \mid x)$: the weight
cancels the density of the assignment mechanism exactly, and what survives is the covariate
average that defines the target. The two Dirac masses in~\eqref{eq:ipw-identity} are the same
ones that appear in the influence function~\eqref{eq:eif-dirac}, and they are regularized the
same way, by $K_{h_T}$ and $K_{h_Y}$; replacing the expectation by a sample average and $\gps$
by an estimate then gives
\begin{equation}
\widehat p^{\,\mathrm{ipw}}_t(y)
\;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \omega_i\, K_{h_Y}(Y_i - y) ,
\qquad
\omega_i \;:=\; \frac{K_{h_T}(T_i-t)}{\widehat\gps(t \mid X_i)} .
\label{eq:est-ipw}
\end{equation}
It uses no outcome model at all: it reweights the observed sample so that the treatment
behaves as though it had been randomized. Each unit contributes its own outcome, smoothed by
$K_{h_Y}$ and weighted by how close its treatment is to $t$ divided by how likely it was to
receive that treatment given its covariates, so that units unlikely to be treated at $t$ are
correspondingly up-weighted. That last feature is also its weakness: where $\gps(t\mid x)$ is
small the weight is large, a handful of observations dominate the average, and the variance
degrades. Here $K_{h_T}$ concentrates the sum on the few units whose
treatment lands within $h_T$ of $t$, and a vector treatment makes those few fewer still. The
classical estimator~\eqref{eq:est-classical} is the third, and regrouping its summand makes
the construction visible:
\begin{equation}
\widehat p^{\,\mathrm{c}}_t(y)
\;=\;
\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \widehat\eta(X_i)}_{\text{plug-in}}
\;+\;
\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \omega_i
\big( K_{h_Y}(Y_i-y) - \widehat\eta(X_i) \big)}_{\text{augmentation}} ,
\label{eq:est-classical-augmented}
\end{equation}
the plug-in, plus inverse-probability weighting applied to the \emph{residuals} of the outcome
model rather than to the outcomes themselves. Table~\ref{tab:three-estimators} collects the
three side by side.

Why the augmentation buys robustness can be read off~\eqref{eq:est-classical-augmented}
directly: $\widehat\eta$ enters twice with opposite signs, while $\widehat\gps$ enters only as
a multiplier of a residual. Take each nuisance in turn, in the population and with the
smoothing bias set aside.

Suppose first that $\widehat\eta$ is correct. Because the weight carries $K_{h_T}(T_i-t)$, the
residual is evaluated where $T$ is close to $t$, and there
$\pE\big[K_{h_Y}(Y-y) \,\big|\, T=t, X\big] = \eta(X)$: the augmentation averages a residual of
mean zero and vanishes in expectation. The estimator collapses onto the plug-in, which is
consistent. A wrong $\widehat\gps$ does no damage, because it multiplies a quantity that is
already centred.

Suppose instead that $\widehat\gps$ is correct. Then $\pE[\omega \mid X] = 1$, so the weights
integrate to one within each covariate stratum. The first half of the augmentation recovers
the target, $\pE\big[\omega\,K_{h_Y}(Y-y)\big] = p_t(y)$, and the second satisfies
$\pE\big[\omega\,\widehat\eta(X)\big] = \pE_X\big[\widehat\eta(X)\big]$, which is exactly the
plug-in term with the opposite sign. The estimator returns
$p_t(y) + \pE_X[\widehat\eta] - \pE_X[\widehat\eta] = p_t(y)$: a wrong $\widehat\eta$ cancels
between its two occurrences.

Either nuisance may therefore be wrong, provided the other is not, which is
Proposition~\ref{prop:dr-classical} in qualitative form; the quantitative form, the product
structure of the remainder, is what additionally permits one to converge slowly when the other
converges fast. When both are correct the augmentation does one thing more: subtracting
$\widehat\eta(X_i)$ before weighting acts as a control variate for the weighted average
that~\eqref{eq:est-ipw} leaves bare, reducing its variance, and the estimator attains the
semiparametric efficiency bound. This is the structure that Section~\ref{sec:discussion-dr}
reports as lost once the correction is recomputed through the contrastive factorization:
there, no pair of nuisances remains to play these two roles against each other.

\subsection{The cost of the correction}
\label{sec:eif-cost}

The estimator~\eqref{eq:est-classical} works, but it comes at a price, and naming the price is
what motivates the rest of the paper. The correction has introduced a second nuisance, the
generalized propensity score $\gps$. For a vector treatment $\gps$ is a conditional density on
$\R^{d}$, exactly the kind of object whose rate degrades with the dimension of the conditioning
set, as Section~\ref{sec:setting-plugin} discussed for $\eta$ itself. The correction has
therefore reintroduced, as a second nuisance, the very difficulty that a well-chosen estimator
of $\eta$ would need to sidestep in the first place. Double robustness would let one nuisance
be slow if the other were fast, but here $\gps$ and $\eta$ are both conditional densities in
the treatment coordinate, and both are slow, so the guarantee is empty in the regime that
matters.

There is a way out, and it has two steps. The first is to choose the estimator of $\eta$ so
that no density on $\cT\times\cX$ is ever fitted, which is the subject of the next
subsection. The second is to notice that such an estimator does not merely estimate $\eta$ but
rewrites it, and that the target can then be differentiated through that rewriting rather than
through the back-door form~\eqref{eq:target}; that is Section~\ref{sec:mcd-correction}. The
influence function is unique, but the form one differentiates is not, and the second form does
not contain $\gps$.

\subsection{Marginal contrastive discrimination}
\label{sec:mcd}

The diagnosis above is a specification for the estimator of $\eta$ we should want: one that
never fits a density on $\cT\times\cX$ in the first place, so that the correction has no
high-dimensional conditional density to reintroduce. Marginal contrastive discrimination
\citep{meziani2025mcd} is such an estimator, and it is the one we adopt for the rest of the
paper. This subsection introduces it on its own terms, as a conditional-density estimator;
Section~\ref{sec:mcd-correction} then puts its particular construction to a use it was not
designed for.

Its central idea is to turn the estimation of $\eta$, a conditional density on a
$(d+p)$-dimensional conditioning set, into two easier tasks: a univariate density estimation
problem, for the marginal $\pf{Y}$, and a binary classification problem, whose solution
recovers the density ratio between $\eta$ and $\pf{Y}$. On problems of this shape the method
is competitive with state-of-the-art conditional-density estimators at lower cost; we refer to
\citet{meziani2025mcd} for those comparisons. We use it with conditioning variable $(T,X)$ and
target variable $Y$. It starts from the elementary factorization
\begin{equation}
\pf{Y \mid T=t,X=x}(y)
\;=\; \pf{Y}(y) \cdot \DensityRatio(t,x,y),
\qquad
\DensityRatio(t,x,y) := \frac{\pf{T,X,Y}(t,x,y)}{\pf{T,X}(t,x)\,\pf{Y}(y)} ,
\label{eq:reformulation}
\end{equation}
valid when $\pf{T,X}(t,x) > 0$ and $\pf{Y}(y) > 0$. The first factor is a univariate marginal;
the second is a density ratio that equals one when $Y$ is independent of $(T,X)$ and otherwise
measures their dependence. The classifier learns this ratio.

\begin{mydef}[Marginal contrast function, $\MCF(\ratio)$]
\label{def:MCF}
For a mixture ratio $\ratio \in (0,1)$,
\begin{equation}
\contrast(t,x,y)
\;=\; \frac{\ratio\,\pf{T,X,Y}(t,x,y)}
{\ratio\,\pf{T,X,Y}(t,x,y) + (1-\ratio)\,\pf{T,X}(t,x)\,\pf{Y}(y)} .
\label{eq:contrast}
\end{equation}
\end{mydef}

Definition~\ref{def:MCF} is best read as a posterior probability. Consider the experiment in
which a label $Z\in\{0,1\}$ is drawn with $\pP(Z=1)=\ratio$; when $Z=1$ one records an
observed vector $W=(T,X,Y)$, and when $Z=0$ one records $W=(T,X,\widetilde Y)$ with
$\widetilde Y \sim \pf{Y}$ drawn independently of $(T,X)$. Then $\contrast(t,x,y)$ is exactly
$\pP[Z=1 \mid W=(t,x,y)]$, so recovering the contrast function is a supervised classification
problem: tell a genuine triple from one whose outcome has been swapped for an independent
draw. In practice one builds a contrastive dataset
$\mDwz{N}=\{(W_i,Z_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$, the condition $\MDC(\ratio)$ of \citet{meziani2025mcd},
by labelling each observed vector $Z=1$ and adjoining vectors with resampled outcomes
labelled $Z=0$.

The classifier and the density ratio encode the same information, so either recovers the
other, and hence the conditional density. This is the content of the next identity, which we
use repeatedly.

\begin{mypropo}[Reconstruction identity]
\label{prop:reconstruction}
For all $(t,x,y)$ with $\pf{T,X}(t,x) > 0$ and $\pf{Y}(y) > 0$,
\begin{equation}
\pf{Y \mid T=t,X=x}(y) \;=\; \pf{Y}(y)\,\DensityRatio(t,x,y),
\qquad
\DensityRatio(t,x,y) \;=\; \frac{1-\ratio}{\ratio}\,
\frac{\contrast(t,x,y)}{1-\contrast(t,x,y)} .
\label{eq:reconstruction}
\end{equation}
\end{mypropo}

This is Lemma~1 of \citet{meziani2025mcd}, specialized to conditioning variable $(T,X)$; we
reproduce the short proof in Appendix~\ref{app:mcd-reconstruction} for completeness. The
mixture ratio $\ratio$ is a design parameter: different values give different contrast
functions but the same density ratio, provided the value used to build $\mDwz{N}$ is the one
substituted into~\eqref{eq:reconstruction}.

A contrast estimate $\contrasthat$ and a marginal estimate $\pfhat{Y}$ then combine, through
the second identity in~\eqref{eq:reconstruction}, into an estimate of the conditional density,
\begin{equation}
\widehat\eta(x) \;=\; \pfhat{Y}(y)\,\widehat{\DensityRatio}(t,x,y),
\qquad
\widehat{\DensityRatio} \;=\; \frac{1-\ratio}{\ratio}\,
\frac{\contrasthat}{1-\contrasthat} .
\label{eq:eta-mcd}
\end{equation}
Nothing forces $\widehat\eta$ to integrate to one in $y$; \citet{meziani2025mcd} restore this
by a rescaling step, which we adopt. Substituting~\eqref{eq:eta-mcd} into the
plug-in~\eqref{eq:plugin} already gives a usable estimator of $p_t(y)$, and it is the one the
first-order bias of Section~\ref{sec:setting-plugin} applies to; everything that follows is
about correcting it.

Two features of the construction are worth flagging now, because the whole of
Section~\ref{sec:mcd-correction} turns on them, one favourably and one not. Favourably: because
the classifier only ever compares observed vectors with recombined ones, it works on the
support of the joint law rather than the ambient space, which is why the construction stays
feasible when $d+p$ is large \citep{meziani2025mcd}, and why $\eta$ is recovered without any
density on $\cT\times\cX$ ever being fitted. Unfavourably: the negative class is built from the
data by recombination, so the classifier's training law is itself a functional of $F$.
Perturbing $F$ therefore perturbs what the classifier was trained on, and its influence
function will not be the textbook one.
\section{Correcting the plug-in through the MCD factorization}
\label{sec:mcd-correction}

Section~\ref{sec:eif} produced a correct correction and then showed its cost, and
Section~\ref{sec:mcd} committed to an estimator of $\eta$ chosen so that the cost need not be
paid. The two halves now meet. The observation this section rests on is that marginal
contrastive discrimination does not merely \emph{estimate} $\eta$; it \emph{rewrites} it,
as~\eqref{eq:reformulation}, into a univariate marginal times a density ratio. That rewriting
is a new expression for the target, and the target can be differentiated through it.

The influence function is unique, but the form one differentiates is not. Section~\ref{sec:eif}
differentiated the target in its back-door form~\eqref{eq:target}, in which the conditional
density appears as a single object, and the derivative produced the generalized propensity
score. Substituting~\eqref{eq:reformulation} instead gives the same functional written in terms
of $\pf{Y}$ and $\DensityRatio$, and differentiating \emph{that} gives the same influence
function in a different, equal expression. In this one, as
Section~\ref{sec:mcd-form} shows, $\gps$ does not appear at all. This is the paper's central
move, and it is available only because the nuisance estimator has this particular structure.
The rest of the section builds the estimator that the new expression supports, and computes the
one object it requires.

That object is the price. It is worth setting out the balance sheet before the derivations,
since both sides of it recur throughout the paper.

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{What is gained.} The generalized propensity score disappears from the correction,
and with it the $d$-dimensional kernel $K_{h_T}$ that the classical
estimator~\eqref{eq:est-classical} needs in order to use it: the corrected estimator smooths
only in the outcome coordinate. No density on $\cT\times\cX$ is fitted at any point, by the
plug-in or by the correction. Section~\ref{sec:discussion-simpler} argues that this is a
difference in kind rather than in constant, and that it grows with $d$.
\item \emph{What is paid.} Three things. The correction now requires the influence function of
the \emph{fitted contrast function}, and because the contrastive training sample is built from
the data, this is not the classical sandwich formula but a three-channel object
(Section~\ref{sec:mcd-if-classifier}) that must be derived from scratch. Double robustness is
lost: the two legs of the identified functional have been replaced by a single fitted object,
so no second nuisance remains whose good behaviour could rescue a poor contrast fit
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion-dr}). And the correction is confined to the span of the contrast
model's own Jacobian, so it cannot repair a misspecification lying outside it.
\end{itemize}

The order of what follows is: the data objects the construction needs
(Section~\ref{sec:setting-mcd}), the contrastive form of the influence function and the
estimator it supports (Sections~\ref{sec:mcd-form} and~\ref{sec:mcd-estimator-form}), why the
contrast function's own influence function is not standard
(Section~\ref{sec:mcd-if-classifier}), its derivation for an arbitrary differentiable
discriminant, in closed form, together with the resulting algorithm
(Sections~\ref{sec:mcd-general-classifier}--\ref{sec:mcd-algorithm-generic}).
Sections~\ref{sec:mcd-logistic} and~\ref{sec:mcd-mlp} are then applications of the general
derivation to two specific discriminants.

\subsection{Data and cross-fitting}
\label{sec:setting-mcd}

We first fix the data objects the construction uses. Let
\begin{equation*}
\Dobs
 := \left\{W_i=(T_i,X_i,Y_i):i=1,\ldots,n\right\}
\end{equation*}
denote the original observations. In the cross-fitted procedure, we write
\begin{equation*}
\Dobs=\Dfit\,\dot\cup\,\Deval,
\end{equation*}
where $\Dfit$ is used to estimate the nuisance functions and $\Deval$ is reserved for
evaluating their influence functions; the roles are exchanged and the two resulting estimates
averaged. Applying the contrastive construction of Section~\ref{sec:mcd} to $\Dfit$ gives
\begin{equation*}
\Dctr
 := \left\{(W_k,Z_k):k=1,\ldots,N_{\mathrm{ctr}}\right\},
\qquad Z_k\in\{0,1\},
\end{equation*}
where $Z_k=1$ denotes a genuine observation and $Z_k=0$ a recombined one. Both discriminants of
Sections~\ref{sec:mcd-logistic} and~\ref{sec:mcd-mlp} use this single contrastive training set.
The marginal density is estimated from the original outcomes in $\Dfit$,
\begin{equation*}
\widehat p_Y
\;\leftarrow\;
\left\{Y_i:W_i\in\Dfit\right\} ,
\end{equation*}
the recombined rows in $\Dctr$ being used for classification, not as additional observations in
this estimate. In the original MCD implementation, where no outer cross-fitting is used, the
corresponding kernel density estimate uses all original outcomes $\{Y_i\}_{i=1}^n$.

\subsection{The contrastive form of the correction}
\label{sec:mcd-form}

We begin with the central result of the paper: the same influence function,
recomputed from the contrastive factorization, no longer contains the generalized propensity
score. Substituting the reconstruction identity~\eqref{eq:reconstruction} into the
target~\eqref{eq:target} factorizes it into a univariate marginal and a covariate-averaged
density ratio,
\begin{equation}
p_t(y) \;=\; \pf{Y}(y)\,m(t,y),
\qquad
m(t,y) \;:=\; \pE_X\big[\DensityRatio(t,X,y)\big] .
\label{eq:factorization}
\end{equation}
Both factors are functionals of $F$, and both are estimated from the same data, so a
perturbation of $F$ moves them together. Differentiating this product with the calculus of
Appendix~\ref{app:toolbox} gives the following; the derivation is in
Appendix~\ref{app:mcd-derivation}.

\begin{mypropo}[Contrastive form]
\label{prop:if-mcd}
Under the conditions of Appendix~\ref{app:mcd}, with $\contrast$ evaluated at $(t,X,y)$ inside
the expectations,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\IF\big(w;p_t(y),F\big)
&= \pf{Y}(y)\left\{
\DensityRatio(t,x_w,y) - m(t,y)
+ \frac{1-\ratio}{\ratio}\,
\pE_X\!\left[ \frac{\IF\big(w;\contrast,F\big)}{\big(1-\contrast\big)^{2}} \right]
\right\} \\
&\quad + m(t,y)\Big( \delta_{y_w}(y) - \pf{Y}(y) \Big).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:if-mcd}
\end{equation}
\end{mypropo}

Each group of terms in~\eqref{eq:if-mcd} has a clear provenance, and knowing where it comes
from is what makes the formula usable. The first two terms are the ordinary centred
contribution of the covariate average, now written for the density ratio rather than the
conditional density directly. The bracketed expectation carries the influence function of the
contrast function itself, and appears because the density ratio is not observed but fitted; it
is the whole subject of Section~\ref{sec:mcd-if-classifier}. The final term is the correction
attached to the univariate marginal, and it is the only place where a point evaluation
survives: the outcome is read off at $y$ inside $\pf{Y}(y)$, and the corresponding Dirac is
regularized by $K_{h_Y}$ exactly as in Section~\ref{sec:eif-classical}. Crucially, no Dirac
appears in the treatment argument, because the contrast function is evaluated at $(t,X,y)$
through a fitted model rather than through a point mass of the data.

The generalized propensity score is absent from~\eqref{eq:if-mcd}. The conditional outcome
density is still present, by the reconstruction identity of
Proposition~\ref{prop:reconstruction}, $\pf{Y}(y)\,\DensityRatio(t,x_w,y)$ is exactly that
density, but it enters only through the two factors of~\eqref{eq:reformulation}, a univariate
marginal and a contrast function, neither of which is a conditional density in the treatment
coordinate. The influence function is unique, so this contrastive form and the back-door
form~\eqref{eq:eif-dirac} are two expressions of the same object; we work from the contrastive
one, in which the propensity score never appears, and build the IF-corrected estimator around it.

\subsection{The IF-corrected estimator}
\label{sec:mcd-estimator-form}

The contrastive influence function of Proposition~\ref{prop:if-mcd} is all that the one-step
construction of Section~\ref{sec:eif-vm} needs. Adding the empirical average of the estimated
influence function to the plug-in, and regularizing the one surviving Dirac by $K_{h_Y}$ as in
Section~\ref{sec:eif-classical}, gives the \emph{IF-corrected estimator}
\begin{equation}
\widehat p^{\,\mathrm{ifc}}_t(y)
= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left\{
\pfhat{Y}(y)\Big[
\widehat{\DensityRatio}(t,X_i,y) - \widehat m(t,y)
+ \frac{1-\ratio}{\ratio}\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}
\frac{\widehat{\IF}\big(W_i;\contrast(t,X_j,y)\big)}{\big(1-\contrasthat(t,X_j,y)\big)^{2}}
\Big]
+ \widehat m(t,y)\, K_{h_Y}(Y_i - y)
\right\},
\label{eq:estimator-general}
\end{equation}
with $\widehat m(t,y) = n^{-1}\sum_{j} \widehat{\DensityRatio}(t,X_j,y)$. Two properties are
already visible, and together they are the payoff of the whole contrastive route: the estimator
uses no generalized propensity score, and its only bandwidth is $h_Y$, tied to the point
evaluation of the outcome, so no smoothing is performed in the treatment coordinate.

Only one ingredient remains unspecified: $\widehat{\IF}\big(W_i;\contrast\big)$, the influence
function of the fitted contrast function. Everything else in~\eqref{eq:estimator-general} is
supplied by the MCD density estimator of Section~\ref{sec:mcd}, the marginal
$\pfhat{Y}$, the density ratio $\widehat{\DensityRatio}$, and their covariate average
$\widehat m$. That last object is not a standard model influence function, because the classifier
is trained on a sample built from the data, and estimating it is the subject of the rest of
this section. Writing down~\eqref{eq:estimator-general} in full therefore requires computing
$\IF(w;\contrast,F)$ for the classifier at hand, and its expression depends on that classifier:
Sections~\ref{sec:mcd-logistic} and~\ref{sec:mcd-mlp} carry out this computation in turn for
penalized logistic regression and for a fixed-architecture multilayer perceptron.

\subsection{The influence function of the contrast estimate}
\label{sec:mcd-if-classifier}

The IF-corrected estimator~\eqref{eq:estimator-general} hinges on the one object we deferred,
$\IF(w;\contrast,F)$, the derivative of the fitted contrast function with respect to the
data-generating law. Influence functions of trained models are not new, and it helps to say why
the standard formula does not apply here. For penalized $M$-estimators the influence function is the classical sandwich
expression, treated in modern form by \citet{avella2017}. In the machine-learning literature
\citet{kohliang2017} reintroduced influence functions as a tool for interpreting trained
predictors, and their behaviour in that setting was studied by \citet{basu2021} and
\citet{bae2022}. Every one of these formulas describes the effect of perturbing a
\emph{fixed} training distribution.

The contrastive construction breaks that assumption. The negative class of $\mDwz{N}$ is
generated from the observed data, so the law of the training sample depends on $F$ through two
of its marginals. Perturbing $F$ therefore perturbs the training distribution as well as the
evaluation distribution, and the derivative acquires terms the classical formula does not have.
To make this precise, write the two component laws of the contrastive experiment as
\begin{equation}
P_1(F) := F_{T,X,Y}, \qquad
P_0(F) := F_{T,X} \otimes F_Y ,
\label{eq:laws}
\end{equation}
and let $\pE_{P_\ratio} := \ratio\,\pE_{P_1} + (1-\ratio)\,\pE_{P_0}$ be expectation under the
mixture prescribed by $\MDC(\ratio)$. The positive law $P_1(F)$ is $F$ itself, but the negative
law $P_0(F)$ is a \emph{product} of two marginals of $F$, and a contamination of $F$ perturbs
each factor of that product separately. It is this product structure that produces the extra
channels, as each discriminant treated below will show explicitly.

\subsection{A common differentiable classifier form}
\label{sec:mcd-general-classifier}

This section fixes the class of discriminants the rest of the paper corrects, and derives its
influence function once, for the whole class at once. Sections~\ref{sec:mcd-logistic}
and~\ref{sec:mcd-mlp} then instantiate it.

\begin{mydef}[Differentiable discriminant]
\label{def:diff-discriminant}
A \emph{differentiable discriminant} is a classifier
\begin{equation}
D_\beta(w)=\sigma\big(F_\beta(w)\big),
\qquad
\sigma(u)=(1+e^{-u})^{-1},
\label{eq:general-logit}
\end{equation}
fit by minimizing the penalized log-loss risk
\begin{equation}
\cR_\lambda(\beta;F)
=\pE_{P_\ratio}\big[\ell(D_\beta(W),Z)\big]
+\lambda\|\beta\|_2^2,
\qquad
\ell(D,z)=-z\log D-(1-z)\log(1-D),
\qquad
\beta_\lambda(F) := \arg\min_\beta \cR_\lambda(\beta;F),
\label{eq:criterion}
\end{equation}
where the logit $F_\beta:\cW\to\R$ is differentiable in $\beta$, with
$J_\beta(w):=\nabla_\beta F_\beta(w)$, and $\lambda \ge 0$.
\end{mydef}

Two choices in Definition~\ref{def:diff-discriminant} are fixed for the whole class: the link
$\sigma$ and the loss $\ell$. They enter the derivation below through a single cancellation,
$\nabla_\beta D_\beta = D_\beta(1-D_\beta)J_\beta$ combined with
$\partial_D\ell = (D-z)/(D(1-D))$; a probit link or a hinge loss would give a different
cancellation, and Theorem~\ref{thm:channels} below would have to be rederived for it. The
architecture of $F_\beta$ is what varies: logistic regression (Section~\ref{sec:mcd-logistic})
and the multilayer perceptron (Section~\ref{sec:mcd-mlp}) are two instances of
Definition~\ref{def:diff-discriminant}, and any other differentiable network would be a third.
A classifier without a well-defined $\nabla_\beta F_\beta$, a decision tree or a
gradient-boosted ensemble grown by greedy splits, falls outside
Definition~\ref{def:diff-discriminant} altogether; correcting one requires reducing it to the
definition first, by freezing its non-differentiable structure and fitting only a
differentiable head on top of it.

Write $G(\beta,F):=\nabla_\beta\cR_\lambda(\beta;F)$, so that an interior minimizer solves
\begin{equation}
G\big(\beta_\lambda(F),F\big)=0 ,
\label{eq:general-foc}
\end{equation}
and $H_\lambda := \nabla_\beta^2\cR_\lambda(\beta_\lambda;F)$ for the Hessian at that point.

\begin{assumption}[Regularity]
\label{as:regularity}
$\lambda>0$; $\beta_\lambda(F)$ is an interior stationary point of $\cR_\lambda(\cdot;F)$;
$F_\beta$ is twice continuously differentiable in $\beta$ in a neighbourhood of
$\beta_\lambda(F)$; the Hessian $H_\lambda$ is invertible; and $D_{\beta_\lambda}$ is bounded
away from $0$ and $1$ on the support of $P_\ratio$.
\end{assumption}

Assumption~\ref{as:regularity} is what the implicit function theorem needs on top of
Definition~\ref{def:diff-discriminant}, and no more. The two discriminants below differ in
exactly one respect: whether it is a consequence of the specification or a hypothesis about the
fitted point. For an affine logit it is a proposition (Section~\ref{sec:mcd-logistic}); for a
network it must be posited (Section~\ref{sec:mcd-mlp}).

The chain rule for the logistic loss gives $\nabla_\beta\ell(D_\beta(w),z)
=\big(D_\beta(w)-z\big)J_\beta(w)$, so that with
\begin{equation}
u_\beta(w):=\big(D_\beta(w)-1\big)J_\beta(w),
\qquad
v_\beta(w):=D_\beta(w)J_\beta(w)
\label{eq:general-uv}
\end{equation}
the gradient of the loss at labels $1$ and $0$ is $u_\beta$ and $v_\beta$ respectively.
Differentiating the first-order condition~\eqref{eq:general-foc} along the contamination
path~\eqref{eq:perturbation} gives the central result of this section.

\begin{theorem}[Influence function of the contrast estimate]
\label{thm:channels}
Under Assumption~\ref{as:regularity}, with $\beta = \beta_\lambda(F)$ and $w=(t_w,x_w,y_w)$,
\begin{equation}
\IF\big(w;\beta_\lambda,F\big) \;=\; -\,H_\lambda^{-1}\,\score(w;\beta),
\qquad
\score \;=\; \score_1 + \score_0^{TX} + \score_0^{Y},
\label{eq:master}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\score_1 &= \ratio\Big( u_\beta(w) - \pE_{P_1}[u_\beta] \Big), \\
\score_0^{TX} &= (1-\ratio)\Big(
\pE_{\widetilde Y \sim F_Y}\big[ v_\beta(t_w,x_w,\widetilde Y) \big]
- \pE_{P_0}[v_\beta] \Big), \\
\score_0^{Y} &= (1-\ratio)\Big(
\pE_{(T,X) \sim F_{T,X}}\big[ v_\beta(T,X,y_w) \big]
- \pE_{P_0}[v_\beta] \Big).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:channels}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The three-channel form is the point of departure from the classical sandwich formula. The
proof, in Appendices~\ref{app:logistic-channels} and~\ref{app:logistic-ift}, applies the
implicit function theorem to~\eqref{eq:general-foc} and then differentiates the mixture law
channel by channel. The interpretation follows the source of each channel. The first,
$\score_1$, is the ordinary score of an $M$-estimator, computed under the law of the observed
data; it is the only channel a fixed training distribution would produce. The other two arise
entirely from the contrastive law: since $P_0(F)$ is a product of two marginals of $F$,
contaminating $F$ contaminates each factor separately, and the derivative of the product
contributes one term per factor. These two channels are what the formulas of
\citet{avella2017} and \citet{kohliang2017} lack. They also disappear when the negative class
is drawn from a distribution fixed by the analyst, as in noise-contrastive estimation
\citep{gutmann2010nce}; in that case~\eqref{eq:master} collapses to the classical sandwich
formula, as verified in Appendix~\ref{app:logistic-centering}.

Nothing in~\eqref{eq:channels} refers to the parameterization of the logit: the score is a
property of the contrastive law alone. The Hessian is where the parameterization enters.
Differentiating $\big(D_\beta-z\big)J_\beta$ once more in $\beta$ gives
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\beta\big[(D_\beta-z)J_\beta\big]
=D_\beta(1-D_\beta)J_\beta J_\beta^\top
+(D_\beta-z)\nabla_\beta^2F_\beta ,
\label{eq:general-hessian-pointwise}
\end{equation}
and averaging over $P_\ratio$ splits the Hessian into a Gauss--Newton part and a curvature
part,
\begin{equation}
H_\lambda=H_\lambda^{\mathrm{GN}}+H_\lambda^{\mathrm{curv}},
\qquad
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
H_\lambda^{\mathrm{GN}} &=\pE_{P_\ratio}\big[D_\beta(1-D_\beta)J_\beta J_\beta^\top\big]+2\lambda I, \\
H_\lambda^{\mathrm{curv}} &=\pE_{P_\ratio}\big[(D_\beta-Z)\nabla_\beta^2F_\beta\big] ,
\end{aligned}
\right.
\label{eq:general-hessian-split}
\end{equation}
the generalized Gauss--Newton split of the Hessian of a model with a sigmoid link and a loss
convex in $D$ \citep{martens2020}. The first part is positive definite for free; the second
carries no sign and vanishes identically when $F_\beta$ is affine in $\beta$. This single
display is the whole difference between the two discriminants below, and
Section~\ref{sec:mcd-mlp} is largely an account of its consequences.

\subsection{Closed form of the three channels}
\label{sec:mcd-general-closed-form}

Evaluating $\score_0^{TX}$ and $\score_0^{Y}$ appears to require two nested expectations for
every $w$. It does not, and the reason is the same for every differentiable discriminant:
because $\nabla_\beta D_\beta = D_\beta(1-D_\beta)J_\beta$, the factor $D_\beta(1-D_\beta)$
cancels against the derivative of the logistic loss, and each channel reduces to a plain
expectation of the already-computed quantity $D_\beta J_\beta$. Define the three averaged
Jacobians
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
b_{TX}(t_w,x_w) &:= \pE_{\widetilde Y \sim \pf{Y}}\big[ D_\beta J_\beta (t_w,x_w,\widetilde Y) \big],
&\qquad
b_Y(y_w) &:= \pE_{(T,X) \sim \pf{T,X}}\big[ D_\beta J_\beta (T,X,y_w) \big], \\
b_0 &:= \pE_{P_0}\big[ D_\beta J_\beta \big].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:b-channels}
\end{equation}

\begin{mypropo}[Closed form]
\label{prop:closed-form}
Under Assumption~\ref{as:regularity},
\begin{equation}
\score(w;\beta)
= \ratio\big( D_\beta(w) - 1 \big)J_\beta(w)
+ (1-\ratio)\Big( b_{TX}(t_w,x_w) + b_Y(y_w) - b_0 \Big)
+ 2\lambda\beta_\lambda .
\label{eq:S}
\end{equation}
\end{mypropo}

The derivation is in Appendix~\ref{app:logistic-centering}. Each of $b_{TX}$ and $b_Y$ is a
Monte Carlo average over draws resampled from a marginal, with no differentiation through the
resampling step, which is what makes~\eqref{eq:S} computable at all. One feature of it is easy
to get wrong in an implementation, and the next lemma guards against it: the three channels
produce two contrastive centring terms, but the first-order
condition~\eqref{eq:general-foc} eliminates only one of them, so a single uncancelled term
$-(1-\ratio)b_0$ survives.

\begin{lem}[Zero mean]
\label{lem:zero-mean}
$\pE_{W \sim F}\big[\score(W;\beta_\lambda)\big] = 0$, and hence
$\pE_{W \sim F}\big[\IF(W;\beta_\lambda,F)\big] = 0$.
\end{lem}

Appendix~\ref{app:logistic-zero-mean} proves this using the fact that when $W \sim F$ the pair
$(T,X)$ follows $F_{T,X}$ and the outcome follows $F_Y$, so each contrastive channel averages
to $b_0$ and the residual is exactly what makes the total vanish. Dropping the term
$-(1-\ratio)b_0$ would leave a nonzero mean, which no influence function can have; the lemma
therefore also serves as a numerical check on any implementation.

\subsection{The correction in closed form}
\label{sec:mcd-general-collapse}

We can now return to the correction term left general in~\eqref{eq:estimator-general} and put
it in closed form. That term needs $\IF(w;\contrast,F)$, the influence function of the fitted
classifier evaluated pointwise at $(t,X,y)$, whereas Theorem~\ref{thm:channels} supplies
$\IF(w;\beta_\lambda,F)$, the influence function of the parameter: a single vector. The link
between them is what makes the closed form possible. Once $\contrast$ is modelled as
$D_{\beta_\lambda(F)}$, only $\beta_\lambda(F)$ is estimated from data, and the map
$\beta\mapsto D_\beta(t,x,y)$, at any fixed $(t,x,y)$, is a known, non-random function, exactly
like $h$ in Lemma~\ref{lem:chain}. The chain rule for influence functions therefore gives
\begin{equation}
\IF\big(w;\contrast(t,x,y),F\big) = \nabla_\beta D_\beta(t,x,y)^\top\,\IF(w;\beta_\lambda,F) .
\label{eq:if-chain}
\end{equation}
Because $\IF(w;\beta_\lambda,F)$ does not depend on $(t,x,y)$, and so not on the covariates
being averaged over, it factors out of the expectation in the correction term
of~\eqref{eq:estimator-general}. That is what collapses it to an inner product, and it is why
the algorithm only ever has to estimate $\IF(w;\beta_\lambda,F)$ once per observation, never
$\IF(w;\contrast(t,x,y),F)$ separately at every evaluation point.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:collapse}
Let $a_\lambda(t,y) := \pE_X\big[ \DensityRatio(t,X,y)\,J_\beta(t,X,y) \big]$ and
$\xi(t,y) := H_\lambda^{-1}a_\lambda(t,y)$. Then
\begin{equation}
\frac{1-\ratio}{\ratio}\,
\pE_X\!\left[ \frac{\IF\big(w;\contrast(t,X,y),F\big)}{\big(1-\contrast(t,X,y)\big)^{2}} \right]
\;=\; a_\lambda(t,y)^\top\, \IF\big(w;\beta_\lambda,F\big)
\;=\; -\,\xi(t,y)^\top \score(w;\beta) .
\label{eq:collapse}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

Appendix~\ref{app:logistic-collapse} proves it. Combining Proposition~\ref{prop:if-mcd},
Theorem~\ref{thm:channels} and Corollary~\ref{cor:collapse} turns the general
estimator~\eqref{eq:estimator-general} into its closed form,
\begin{equation}
\widehat p^{\,\mathrm{ifc}}_t(y)
= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left\{
\pfhat{Y}(y)\Big[
\widehat{\DensityRatio}(t,X_i,y) - \widehat m(t,y)
- \widehat\xi(t,y)^\top \widehat\score(W_i)
\Big]
+ \widehat m(t,y)\, K_{h_Y}(Y_i - y)
\right\},
\label{eq:estimator}
\end{equation}
with $\widehat m(t,y) = n^{-1}\sum_{j} \widehat{\DensityRatio}(t,X_j,y)$. This is the closed
form promised in Section~\ref{sec:mcd-estimator-form}: the correction term
of~\eqref{eq:estimator-general} has become the single inner product
$\widehat\xi(t,y)^\top\widehat\score(W_i)$, costing one linear solve per evaluation point and
one inner product per observation. It uses no generalized propensity score and smooths only in
the outcome coordinate.

\subsection{The estimation procedure}
\label{sec:mcd-algorithm-generic}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:generic} states the whole procedure. It is complete for any discriminant
satisfying Assumption~\ref{as:regularity}: no step is left open, and
Sections~\ref{sec:mcd-logistic} and~\ref{sec:mcd-mlp} change nothing in it beyond how the two
linear-algebra steps, forming $\widehat H_\lambda$ and solving against it, are carried out.
Cross-fitting \citep{chernozhukov2018} runs throughout, the contrast function being fitted on
one fold and the influence function evaluated on the other, with the roles exchanged and the
two estimates averaged; this removes the empirical-process conditions that would otherwise be
needed and makes the conditional-bias identity~\eqref{eq:plugin-bias} hold exactly.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{The IF-corrected estimator (any differentiable discriminant)}
\label{alg:generic}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Split $\Dobs$ into $\Dfit$ and $\Deval$; exchange the roles at the end and average the two estimates.
\State On $\Dfit$, build $\Dctr$ satisfying $\MDC(\ratio)$ by labelling each observed vector $Z=1$ and adjoining $\kappa$ vectors with resampled outcomes labelled $Z=0$, so that $\ratio=1/(1+\kappa)$.
\State Fit $\betahat_\lambda$ on $\Dctr$ by minimizing~\eqref{eq:criterion}; set $\contrasthat=\sigma(F_{\betahat_\lambda})$ and $\widehat{\DensityRatio}=\tfrac{1-\ratio}{\ratio}\,\contrasthat/(1-\contrasthat)$.
\State Fit $\pfhat{Y}$ from the original outcomes in $\Dfit$.
\State Form $\widehat H_\lambda$ from~\eqref{eq:general-hessian-split} on $\Dctr$ and prepare a solver for it; form $\widehat b_0$ as an average of $D_\beta J_\beta$ over the contrastive part of $\Dctr$.
\For{each $W_i$ in $\Deval$}
  \State compute $\widehat b_{TX}(T_i,X_i)$ and $\widehat b_Y(Y_i)$ by subsampling the corresponding marginals over $S_{\mathrm{mc}}$ draws;
  \State assemble $\widehat\score(W_i)$ from~\eqref{eq:S}.
\EndFor
\For{each evaluation point $(t,y)$}
  \State $\widehat m(t,y)\gets n^{-1}\sum_j\widehat{\DensityRatio}(t,X_j,y)$;
  \State $\widehat a_\lambda(t,y)\gets n^{-1}\sum_j \widehat{\DensityRatio}(t,X_j,y)\,J_{\betahat_\lambda}(t,X_j,y)$; solve $\widehat H_\lambda\,\widehat\xi(t,y)=\widehat a_\lambda(t,y)$.
\EndFor
\State \Return $\widehat p^{\,\mathrm{ifc}}_t(y)$ assembled from~\eqref{eq:estimator}.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Two costs are worth reading off the algorithm, because they are what the next two subsections
trade against each other. Step~5 is paid once, not once per observation: the Hessian is a
single average over $\Dctr$, prepared once and reused at every evaluation point. Step~7 is the
inner Monte Carlo, and subsampling it over $S_{\mathrm{mc}}$ draws brings the loop to $S_{\mathrm{mc}}$ evaluations of
$D_\beta J_\beta$ per observation, at the price of an $\cO(S^{-1/2})$ error inside a term that
is itself averaged over the sample. The
one genuinely open question is step~5's solver, and the answer depends on whether
$H_\lambda^{\mathrm{curv}}$ in~\eqref{eq:general-hessian-split} vanishes.

\subsection{The penalized logistic discriminant}
\label{sec:mcd-logistic}

The first application takes $F_\beta$ affine in $\beta$, the parameterization under which
\citet{meziani2025mcd} establish the Bayes optimality of the contrast function.
Theorem~\ref{thm:channels}, Proposition~\ref{prop:closed-form}, Lemma~\ref{lem:zero-mean} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:collapse} apply verbatim; only the specification below is new.

\paragraph{Specification.}
\label{sec:mcd-logistic-spec}
Let $\phi:\cW\to\R^{\ks}$ be a fixed feature map and set
\begin{equation}
F_\beta(w) = \beta^\top\phi(w),
\qquad\text{so that}\qquad
J_\beta(w) = \phi(w)
\quad\text{and}\quad
\nabla^2_\beta F_\beta \equiv 0 .
\label{eq:logistic-logit}
\end{equation}

\begin{assumption}[Regularity, logistic discriminant]
\label{as:smooth}
$\lambda > 0$, the feature map $\phi$ is bounded on $\cW$, and $D_{\beta_\lambda}$ is bounded
away from $0$ and $1$ on the support of $P_\ratio$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{mypropo}[Assumption~\ref{as:regularity} holds, and globally]
\label{prop:logistic-regularity}
Under Assumption~\ref{as:smooth}, the criterion~\eqref{eq:criterion} is strictly convex, so
$\beta_\lambda(F)$ exists, is unique and is interior; $H_\lambda^{\mathrm{curv}} = 0$; and every
eigenvalue of $H_\lambda$ is at least $2\lambda$, at every $\beta$. Proof in
Appendix~\ref{app:logistic-grads}, which also covers the unpenalized case $\lambda=0$,
admissible provided $\phi$ is not collinear on the support.
\end{mypropo}

\paragraph{Closed form.}
\label{sec:mcd-logistic-closed-form}
Substituting $J_\beta=\phi$, the score~\eqref{eq:S} becomes
\begin{equation}
\score(w;\beta)
= \ratio\big( D_\beta(w) - 1 \big)\phi(w)
+ (1-\ratio)\Big( b_{TX}(t_w,x_w) + b_Y(y_w) - b_0 \Big)
+ 2\lambda\beta_\lambda ,
\label{eq:S-logistic}
\end{equation}
with $a_\lambda(t,y) = \pE_X[\DensityRatio(t,X,y)\,\phi(t,X,y)]$ in Corollary~\ref{cor:collapse}.
Because $\phi$ is fixed, $H_\lambda$ ($\ks\times\ks$) is factorized once per fold, so every
subsequent solve is a triangular solve rather than a fresh $\cO(\ks^3)$ inversion, and
$b_{TX},b_Y$ are Monte Carlo averages of $D_\beta\phi$ with no backward pass in the loop.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
\label{sec:mcd-algorithm-logistic}
\begin{remark}[Implementation]
\label{rem:impl-logistic}
Three choices fix Algorithm~\ref{alg:generic} for Section~\ref{sec:simulation}.
$\widehat H_\lambda$ (step~5) is factorized once per fold by Cholesky decomposition. $\lambda$
defaults to $0.5$ regardless of $\ks$, an order of magnitude above bare well-specification
arguments, after direct measurement (Appendix~\ref{app:cv}) showed a smaller value leaves
$\betahat_\lambda$ overconfident whenever $\phi$ is mildly misspecified, a bias that
propagates through~\eqref{eq:S} into the correction term. The Monte Carlo average defining
$\widehat b_{TX},\widehat b_Y$ (step~7) is computed in batches capped independently of $\ks$, to
bound memory use on a GPU as $\ks$ grows into the thousands (Appendix~\ref{app:compute}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{The neural-network discriminant}
\label{sec:mcd-mlp}

The second application takes $F_\beta$ nonlinear in $\beta$. Theorem~\ref{thm:channels},
Proposition~\ref{prop:closed-form}, Lemma~\ref{lem:zero-mean} and Corollary~\ref{cor:collapse}
hold as written, with $J_\beta$ the network's logit Jacobian; only the Hessian changes.

\paragraph{Specification.}
\label{sec:mcd-mlp-spec}
Let $F_\beta:\cW\to\R$ be a multilayer perceptron of fixed depth and width, $\beta$ collecting
its $P$ weights, and $D_\beta(w)=\sigma(F_\beta(w))$. Then
\begin{equation}
J_\beta(w) = \nabla_\beta F_\beta(w) \in \R^{P},
\qquad
\nabla^2_\beta F_\beta \not\equiv 0 ,
\label{eq:mlp-logit}
\end{equation}
computed by one backward pass; unlike the fixed $\phi$ above, $J_\beta$ depends on $\beta$.

\label{sec:mcd-mlp-ggn}
\begin{assumption}[Local regularity]
\label{as:smooth-mlp}
Assumption~\ref{as:regularity} holds at the fitted stationary point $\beta_\lambda(F)$; in
particular $H_\lambda$ is invertible there.
\end{assumption}

\begin{mypropo}[Gauss--Newton decomposition]
\label{prop:ggn}
$H_\lambda^{\mathrm{GN}} \succeq 2\lambda I \succ 0$ always. $H_\lambda^{\mathrm{curv}} = 0$
whenever $F_\beta$ is affine in $\beta$, and is sign-indefinite in general, the generalized
Gauss--Newton split of a model with a sigmoid link and a convex loss \citep{martens2020}.
\end{mypropo}

Unlike the logistic case, $H_\lambda^{\mathrm{curv}}$ is generically nonzero for a multilayer
$F_\beta$ and carries no sign, which is why Assumption~\ref{as:smooth-mlp} must be posited at
$\beta_\lambda(F)$ rather than derived.

\paragraph{Solving against the Hessian.}
\label{sec:mcd-algorithm-mlp}
$H_\lambda$ is $P\times P$, ordinarily too large to form or factorize; every linear system is
solved by an iterative method using Hessian-vector products alone (the Pearlmutter trick),
never forming $\widehat H_\lambda$, as in \citet{kohliang2017}. The default target is the exact
Hessian, solved by an indefinite-tolerant method (MINRES, or damped conjugate gradients); the
Gauss--Newton matrix $\widehat H_\lambda^{\mathrm{GN}}$ is a cheaper, always-positive-definite
fallback, accurate when the classifier already separates real from recombined vectors well.
Assumption~\ref{as:smooth-mlp} is checked numerically after fitting, from the smallest
eigenvalues of $\widehat H_\lambda$ (Lanczos) and the ratio
$\|\widehat H_\lambda^{\mathrm{curv}}\|/\|\widehat H_\lambda^{\mathrm{GN}}\|$, which decides
whether the cheaper Gauss--Newton solve is trustworthy.

\subsection{Validating the influence function}
\label{sec:sim-if}

Before assembling the estimator, we check that~\eqref{eq:master} really is the influence
function it claims to be: a purely numerical statement, independent of any asymptotic argument,
and worth verifying on its own before any sampling variability is allowed to obscure a possible
error in the formula itself.

We build a finite population $F$ of fixed size (feature map $\phi$ and penalty $\lambda$ fixed
in advance), for which $\beta_\lambda(F)$ is the exact, unique minimizer of the finite-sum
criterion~\eqref{eq:criterion}. For a probe point $w$ and a grid of $\varepsilon \to 0$, $F$ is
perturbed along the contamination path $F_\varepsilon = (1-\varepsilon)F + \varepsilon\delta_w$
of~\eqref{eq:perturbation}, and~\eqref{eq:criterion} is re-solved exactly at every
$\varepsilon$ to form the centred finite difference of $\beta_\lambda(F_\varepsilon)$. This
finite difference is the ground truth against which three predictions are compared, in relative
$L_2$ norm and cosine similarity, as functions of $\varepsilon$: the closed-form three-channel
formula~\eqref{eq:S} of Theorem~\ref{thm:channels}; the single-channel ``classical sandwich''
formula of \citet{avella2017,kohliang2017}, which keeps only $\score_1$ and drops
$\score_0^{TX},\score_0^{Y}$, included as a negative control that must fail once the two
contrastive channels are not negligible; and the mean-zero identity of Lemma~\ref{lem:zero-mean},
tracked as $\varepsilon \to 0$ as a further consistency check. The same three comparisons are
repeated for the neural-network discriminant, where Theorem~\ref{thm:channels} holds with
$J_\beta$ in place of $\phi$, with the
exact-Hessian and Gauss--Newton solves of Proposition~\ref{prop:ggn} evaluated separately, as
functions of $\|\widehat H_\lambda^{\mathrm{curv}}\|/\|\widehat H_\lambda^{\mathrm{GN}}\|$; this
turns Assumption~\ref{as:smooth-mlp}, otherwise unverifiable, into a diagnostic with a
quantitative threshold (Section~\ref{sec:mcd-algorithm-mlp}).

A second layer of the same check moves from the population to a sample: Algorithm~\ref{alg:generic}
is run on samples of size $n$ drawn from $F$, over the $(n,S_{\mathrm{mc}})$ grid of Appendix~\ref{app:cv},
$S_{\mathrm{mc}}$ the Monte Carlo size used for $\widehat b_{TX},\widehat b_Y$, and its output is compared to
the population influence function computed above. An ablation recomputes $\widehat b_{TX},
\widehat b_Y$ \emph{exactly} over the sample's atoms rather than by Monte Carlo subsampling, which
isolates the $\cO(S^{-1/2})$ error contributed by subsampling from the $\cO(n^{-1/2})$ error
already present in a finite sample of size $n$. Throughout, the empirical mean of
$\widehat\score(W_i)$ is tracked against $n$ and $S_{\mathrm{mc}}$, as a running check of Lemma~\ref{lem:zero-mean}
that any correct implementation must pass regardless of the outcome of the comparisons above.

\textbf{AXY} (Appendix~\ref{app:dgp-axy}), adapted from the toy structural model of

Figure~\ref{fig:dgp-toyscm-axy} shows its outcome mechanism.

The \textbf{well-specified linear-Gaussian model} (Appendix~\ref{app:dgp-wellspecified}) is a
control: a Gaussian confounder and a linear-Gaussian outcome, for which the contrast function's
log-odds is an exact quadratic polynomial in $(t,x,y)$, so a degree-two feature map is not an
approximation but an exact representation. AXY and the four models below are all, by design,
misspecified for such a feature map; this one is not, and is what isolates the estimation-only
regime, in which the parametric family contains the contrast function exactly and nothing is
left for an approximation error to contribute, from the approximation-plus-estimation regime
the other designs exercise.

The \textbf{four continuous models} (Appendix~\ref{app:dgp-continuous}) share the paper's actual
regime, confounding jointly Gaussian in $(X,T)$, both dimensions free to vary, and differ
only in the outcome law $Y\mid T,X$, each adding one property a jointly Gaussian $(X,T,Y)$ cannot
produce: a mixing weight that depends on $t$ (multimodal), a Student-$t$ noise term (heavy
tails), a variance that grows with $t$ (heteroscedastic), or a skew-normal noise term (skewed).

qualitative illustration; since $\mathrm{do}(T=t)$ severs $X\to T$, only the outcome mechanism,
never the confounding mechanism, can shape $p_t(y)$, which is why varying it is enough.

Finally, the \textbf{real and semi-synthetic datasets} (IHDP, ACIC 2016/2018, HC-MNIST;

a binary treatment; they are not part of this simulation study, which stays confined to the
IF-corrected estimator and its two discriminants.

\section{Perturbation calculus}
\label{app:toolbox}

Throughout this appendix $F_\varepsilon = (1-\varepsilon)F + \varepsilon\,\delta_w$ is the
contamination path of~\eqref{eq:perturbation}, and $\IF$ is the influence functional of
Definition~\ref{def:if}. The single idea behind every rule below is that
$\varepsilon \mapsto \psi(F_\varepsilon)$ is a real-valued function of one real variable, so
the ordinary rules of differentiation apply to it; each lemma is one such rule, translated into
the notation of $\IF$. We use these lemmas without further comment in
Appendices~\ref{app:classical}--\ref{app:asymptotics}.

\begin{lem}[Linearity]
\label{lem:sum}
For functionals $\psi_1,\psi_2$ admitting influence functions at $F$ and constants
$a,b \in \R$,
\[
\IF\big(w; a\psi_1 + b\psi_2, F\big)
= a\,\IF(w;\psi_1,F) + b\,\IF(w;\psi_2,F).
\]
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
The map $\varepsilon \mapsto a\psi_1(F_\varepsilon) + b\psi_2(F_\varepsilon)$ is a linear
combination of two differentiable functions, so its derivative at $\varepsilon = 0$ is the
same linear combination of the two derivatives. By Definition~\ref{def:if} those derivatives
are $\IF(w;\psi_1,F)$ and $\IF(w;\psi_2,F)$, which gives the claimed identity.
\end{proof}

\begin{lem}[Product]
\label{lem:product}
$\IF\big(w;\psi_1\psi_2,F\big)
= \psi_1(F)\,\IF(w;\psi_2,F) + \psi_2(F)\,\IF(w;\psi_1,F)$.
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
Set $\varphi(\varepsilon) = \psi_1(F_\varepsilon)$ and
$\chi(\varepsilon) = \psi_2(F_\varepsilon)$. The ordinary product rule gives
$(\varphi\chi)'(0) = \varphi(0)\chi'(0) + \chi(0)\varphi'(0)$. Evaluating the four factors at
$\varepsilon = 0$ using Definition~\ref{def:if}, we have $\varphi(0) = \psi_1(F)$,
$\chi(0) = \psi_2(F)$, $\varphi'(0) = \IF(w;\psi_1,F)$ and $\chi'(0) = \IF(w;\psi_2,F)$.
Substituting yields
$\psi_1(F)\,\IF(w;\psi_2,F) + \psi_2(F)\,\IF(w;\psi_1,F)$, as claimed.
\end{proof}

\begin{lem}[Quotient]
\label{lem:quotient}
If $\psi_2(F) \neq 0$,
\[
\IF\!\left(w;\frac{\psi_1}{\psi_2},F\right)
= \frac{\IF(w;\psi_1,F)}{\psi_2(F)}
- \frac{\psi_1(F)}{\psi_2(F)^2}\,\IF(w;\psi_2,F).
\]
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
With $\varphi,\chi$ as in the previous proof and $\chi(0) = \psi_2(F) \neq 0$, the ordinary
quotient rule gives $(\varphi/\chi)'(0) = \{\varphi'(0)\chi(0) - \varphi(0)\chi'(0)\}/\chi(0)^2$.
Substituting the values of $\varphi(0),\chi(0),\varphi'(0),\chi'(0)$ from Definition~\ref{def:if}
and separating the two fractions gives the stated expression.
\end{proof}

\begin{lem}[Chain rule]
\label{lem:chain}
For $h : \R \to \R$ differentiable,
$\IF\big(w;h \circ \psi,F\big) = h'\big(\psi(F)\big)\,\IF(w;\psi,F)$.
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
With $\chi(\varepsilon) = \psi(F_\varepsilon)$, the ordinary chain rule gives
$(h\circ\chi)'(0) = h'(\chi(0))\chi'(0)$. Since $\chi(0) = \psi(F)$ and
$\chi'(0) = \IF(w;\psi,F)$, this is $h'(\psi(F))\,\IF(w;\psi,F)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lem}[Odds transformation]
\label{lem:odds}
Let $\psi$ take values in $(0,1)$ and $h(u) = u/(1-u)$. Then
\[
\IF\big(w;h(\psi),F\big) = \frac{\IF(w;\psi,F)}{\big(1-\psi(F)\big)^2} .
\]
In particular, since $\DensityRatio = \frac{1-\ratio}{\ratio}\, h(\contrast)$ with $\ratio$
constant,
\begin{equation}
\IF\big(w;\DensityRatio,F\big)
= \frac{1-\ratio}{\ratio}\,
\frac{\IF\big(w;\contrast,F\big)}{\big(1-\contrast\big)^{2}} .
\label{eq:app-if-R}
\end{equation}
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
Write $h(\psi) = \psi_1/\psi_2$ with $\psi_1 = \psi$ and $\psi_2 = 1-\psi$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:sum}, $\IF(w;1-\psi,F) = -\IF(w;\psi,F)$. Applying Lemma~\ref{lem:quotient} and
substituting this,
\[
\IF\big(w;h(\psi),F\big)
= \frac{\IF(w;\psi,F)}{1-\psi(F)}
+ \frac{\psi(F)}{(1-\psi(F))^2}\,\IF(w;\psi,F)
= \IF(w;\psi,F)\,\frac{(1-\psi(F)) + \psi(F)}{(1-\psi(F))^2},
\]
and the numerator equals $1$, which gives the first display. For the second, apply
Lemma~\ref{lem:sum} with the constant $\frac{1-\ratio}{\ratio}$ (whose influence function is
zero, being law-independent) to $\DensityRatio = \frac{1-\ratio}{\ratio} h(\contrast)$ and
substitute $\psi = \contrast$ into the first display; this is exactly~\eqref{eq:app-if-R}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lem}[Expectation of a law-dependent integrand]
\label{lem:if-expectation}
Let $h(\cdot\,;F)$ depend on $F$ both through the law of the integration variable and through
its functional form. Then
\begin{equation}
\IF\big(w; \pE_F[h(W;F)], F\big)
= h(w;F) - \pE_F\big[h(W;F)\big]
+ \pE_F\big[ \IF(w;h(\cdot\,;F),F) \big].
\label{eq:app-if-expectation}
\end{equation}
\end{lem}
\begin{proof}
By the definition of $F_\varepsilon$ as a mixture, expectation under $F_\varepsilon$ splits as
\[
\pE_{F_\varepsilon}\big[h(W;F_\varepsilon)\big]
= (1-\varepsilon)\,\pE_F\big[h(W;F_\varepsilon)\big]
+ \varepsilon\,h(w;F_\varepsilon).
\]
Differentiate at $\varepsilon = 0$, term by term. In the first summand the product rule gives
two pieces: differentiating the factor $(1-\varepsilon)$ contributes $-\pE_F[h(W;F)]$, and
differentiating the integrand contributes
$\pE_F[\partial_\varepsilon h(W;F_\varepsilon)|_{0}] = \pE_F[\IF(w;h(\cdot\,;F),F)]$, the
interchange of derivative and expectation being licensed by the regularity assumed where the
lemma is invoked. In the second summand, the factor $\varepsilon$ multiplies the derivative of
$h(w;F_\varepsilon)$, which therefore drops out at $\varepsilon = 0$, while the derivative of
$\varepsilon$ times $h(w;F_\varepsilon)$ contributes $h(w;F)$. Summing the three surviving
pieces gives~\eqref{eq:app-if-expectation}.
\end{proof}

The third term of~\eqref{eq:app-if-expectation} is what makes this lemma more than the standard
formula for the influence function of an expectation: it accounts for the dependence of the
integrand on the law, and is present precisely when the integrand is itself estimated, as the
density ratio is in Section~\ref{sec:mcd-correction}.

\section{The nonparametric correction}
\label{app:classical}

\subsection{Regularity conditions}
\label{app:classical-regularity}

Proposition~\ref{prop:eif} holds under Assumption~\ref{as:identification} together with the
following conditions, which are those of \citet{kennedy2017continuous} and
\citet{kennedy2023density} adapted to the present target. The joint density $\pf{T,X,Y}$ is
bounded and twice continuously differentiable in $(t,y)$ in a neighbourhood of the evaluation
point; the map $y \mapsto \pf{Y}(y)$ is twice continuously differentiable at $y$; and the
kernels $K_T,K_Y$ are symmetric, of second order, with compact support. The bandwidths satisfy
$h_T,h_Y \to 0$, $n h_T^{d} h_Y \to \infty$, and the undersmoothing conditions
$\sqrt{n h_T^d h_Y}\,(h_T^2 + h_Y^2) \to 0$. The smoothness in $(t,y)$ is what allows the point
masses to be replaced by kernels with a controlled bias in
Appendix~\ref{app:classical-kernels}; the undersmoothing conditions make that bias negligible
on the $\sqrt n$ scale.

\subsection{Derivation of the influence function}
\label{app:classical-derivative}

We prove Proposition~\ref{prop:eif}. Write $f_\varepsilon$ for the density of $F_\varepsilon$
and express the target along the contamination path as
\[
p_t(y)(F_\varepsilon)
= \int_{\cX} \frac{f_\varepsilon(t,x,y)\,f_\varepsilon(x)}{f_\varepsilon(t,x)} \dd x .
\]
Because the contamination path is a mixture, $\varepsilon \mapsto f_\varepsilon$ is affine, and
each density derivative at $\varepsilon = 0$ is the difference between the corresponding point
mass and the density:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\partial_\varepsilon f_\varepsilon(t,x,y)\big|_{0} &= \Delta(t,x,y) - \pf{T,X,Y}(t,x,y),\\
\partial_\varepsilon f_\varepsilon(x)\big|_{0} &= \Delta(x) - \pf{X}(x),\\
\partial_\varepsilon f_\varepsilon(t,x)\big|_{0} &= \Delta(t,x) - \pf{T,X}(t,x),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:app-density-derivs}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta(t,x,y) := \delta_{t_w}(t)\,\delta_{x_w}(x)\,\delta_{y_w}(y)$ and analogously for
the marginals. Writing the integrand as $AB/C$ with $A = f_\varepsilon(t,x,y)$,
$B = f_\varepsilon(x)$ and $C = f_\varepsilon(t,x)$, ordinary calculus gives
\[
\partial_\varepsilon\!\left( \frac{AB}{C} \right)
= \frac{\dot A B}{C} + \frac{A \dot B}{C} - \frac{AB\dot C}{C^2}.
\]
Substituting~\eqref{eq:app-density-derivs} at $\varepsilon = 0$ and factoring the common
quantity $\pf{T,X,Y}(t,x,y)\pf{X}(x)/\pf{T,X}(t,x)$ out of the bracket,
\begin{equation}
\partial_\varepsilon\, p_t(y)(F_\varepsilon)\big|_{0}
= \int_{\cX} \frac{\pf{T,X,Y}(t,x,y)\,\pf{X}(x)}{\pf{T,X}(t,x)}
\left[
\frac{\Delta(t,x,y)}{\pf{T,X,Y}(t,x,y)}
+ \frac{\Delta(x)}{\pf{X}(x)}
- \frac{\Delta(t,x)}{\pf{T,X}(t,x)}
- 1
\right] \dd x ,
\label{eq:app-deriv-general}
\end{equation}
the constant $-1$ collecting the three density terms $-1-1+1$ of the three derivatives. We now
evaluate the four contributions in turn.

The term in $\Delta(x)$ contains a point mass only in the integration variable $x$, so the
integral collapses at $x = x_w$:
\[
\int_{\cX} \frac{\pf{T,X,Y}(t,x,y)}{\pf{T,X}(t,x)}\,\delta_{x_w}(x)\dd x
= \frac{\pf{T,X,Y}(t,x_w,y)}{\pf{T,X}(t,x_w)}
= \pf{Y \mid T=t,X=x_w}(y) = \eta(x_w),
\]
the integrand being continuous at $x_w$ by Appendix~\ref{app:classical-regularity}.

The term in $\Delta(t,x,y)$ carries point masses in $t$ and $y$, which are \emph{not}
integration variables and therefore remain as Dirac factors; only $\delta_{x_w}(x)$ is
integrated. Using $\gps(t \mid x) = \pf{T,X}(t,x)/\pf{X}(x)$,
\[
\int_{\cX} \frac{\pf{X}(x)}{\pf{T,X}(t,x)}\,
\delta_{t_w}(t)\delta_{x_w}(x)\delta_{y_w}(y)\dd x
= \delta_{t_w}(t)\,\delta_{y_w}(y)\,\frac{\pf{X}(x_w)}{\pf{T,X}(t,x_w)}
= \frac{\delta_{t_w}(t)\,\delta_{y_w}(y)}{\gps(t \mid x_w)} .
\]

The term in $\Delta(t,x)$ carries a point mass in $t$ only, and integrating out $\delta_{x_w}(x)$
gives, again using the definition of $\gps$,
\[
-\int_{\cX} \frac{\pf{T,X,Y}(t,x,y)\,\pf{X}(x)}{\pf{T,X}(t,x)^2}\,
\delta_{t_w}(t)\delta_{x_w}(x)\dd x
= -\,\delta_{t_w}(t)\,\frac{\pf{T,X,Y}(t,x_w,y)}{\pf{T,X}(t,x_w)}\,\frac{\pf{X}(x_w)}{\pf{T,X}(t,x_w)}\cdot\frac{\pf{T,X}(t,x_w)}{\pf{X}(x_w)}
\]
which simplifies to
$-\,\delta_{t_w}(t)\,\eta(x_w)/\gps(t \mid x_w)$.

The constant term $-1$ integrates against the factored density to $-\pE_X[\eta(X)] = -p_t(y)$
by Definition~\ref{def:target}.

Collecting the four contributions,
\[
\partial_\varepsilon\, p_t(y)(F_\varepsilon)\big|_{0}
= \frac{\delta_{t_w}(t)\,\delta_{y_w}(y)}{\gps(t \mid x_w)}
- \frac{\delta_{t_w}(t)\,\eta(x_w)}{\gps(t \mid x_w)}
+ \eta(x_w) - p_t(y),
\]
and factoring $\delta_{t_w}(t)/\gps(t\mid x_w)$ out of the first two terms yields
\[
\IF\big(w;p_t(y),F\big)
= \frac{\delta_{t_w}(t)}{\gps(t \mid x_w)}\Big(\delta_{y_w}(y) - \eta(x_w)\Big)
+ \eta(x_w) - p_t(y),
\]
which is exactly~\eqref{eq:eif-dirac}, the claim of Proposition~\ref{prop:eif}. \hfill$\square$

\subsection{Regularization of the point evaluations}
\label{app:classical-kernels}

Expression~\eqref{eq:eif-dirac} is exact but not computable, because it contains Dirac masses
in $t$ and $y$. We regularize each by a kernel. Let $K_T : \R^{d} \to \R_+$ and
$K_Y : \R \to \R_+$ be symmetric second-order kernels integrating to one, and for bandwidths
$h_T, h_Y > 0$ set
\begin{equation}
K_{h_T}(u) = h_T^{-d} K_T(u/h_T), \qquad
K_{h_Y}(v) = h_Y^{-1} K_Y(v/h_Y).
\label{eq:kernels}
\end{equation}
Substituting $\delta_{t_w}(t) \rightsquigarrow K_{h_T}(t_w - t)$ and
$\delta_{y_w}(y) \rightsquigarrow K_{h_Y}(y_w - y)$ in~\eqref{eq:eif-dirac}, and leaving the
nuisances unchanged, gives the smoothed influence function
\begin{equation}
\IF_{h}\big(w;p_t(y),F\big)
= \frac{K_{h_T}(t_w-t)}{\gps(t \mid x_w)}
\Big( K_{h_Y}(y_w-y) - \pf{Y \mid T=t,X=x_w}(y) \Big)
+ \pf{Y \mid T=t,X=x_w}(y) - p_t(y),
\label{eq:app-if-smoothed}
\end{equation}
Write out the one-step construction~\eqref{eq:von-mises} at $F=\widehat F$: the plug-in
$\widehat p^{\,\mathrm{pi}}_t(y) = n^{-1}\sum_i\widehat\eta(X_i)$ of~\eqref{eq:plugin}, plus the
empirical average of~\eqref{eq:app-if-smoothed} with the nuisances estimated,
\begin{equation}
\widehat p^{\,\mathrm{pi}}_t(y)
+ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\left[
\frac{K_{h_T}(T_i-t)}{\widehat\gps(t\mid X_i)}\Big(K_{h_Y}(Y_i-y)-\widehat\eta(X_i)\Big)
+ \widehat\eta(X_i) - \widehat p^{\,\mathrm{pi}}_t(y)
\right] .
\label{eq:app-onestep}
\end{equation}
The last two terms inside the sum average, respectively, to $\widehat p^{\,\mathrm{pi}}_t(y)$
itself and to $-\widehat p^{\,\mathrm{pi}}_t(y)$, and these cancel against the leading plug-in
term: the plug-in does not disappear from the construction, it cancels out of the
\emph{display}. What remains of~\eqref{eq:app-onestep} is exactly the classical
estimator~\eqref{eq:est-classical}, with no plug-in term visible in it, which is why
reading~\eqref{eq:est-classical} on its own can look unrelated to the one-step recipe
of~\eqref{eq:von-mises} that produced it. Under the smoothness
and undersmoothing conditions of Appendix~\ref{app:classical-regularity}, a second-order Taylor
expansion of the smoothed functional around $(t,y)$ gives
$\pE\big[\IF_h(W;p_t(y),F)\big] = \cO(h_T^2 + h_Y^2)$, so the substitution introduces a
deterministic bias of that order, which the undersmoothing conditions render negligible on the
$\sqrt n$ scale.

We now derive the bias claim in detail, since it is what licenses the substitution. Because
$\pE_F[\eta(X) - p_t(y)] = 0$ exactly, by Definition~\ref{def:target}, only the first term
of~\eqref{eq:app-if-smoothed} contributes to $\pE_F[\IF_h(W;p_t(y),F)]$, and iterating
expectations over $X$, then $T\mid X$, then $Y\mid T,X$ isolates
\begin{equation}
\pE_F\big[\IF_h(W;p_t(y),F)\big]
= \pE_X\!\left[ \frac{1}{\gps(t\mid X)}\,
\pE_{T\mid X}\Big[ K_{h_T}(T-t)\,\big(\pE_{Y\mid T,X}[K_{h_Y}(Y-y)] - \eta(X)\big) \Big] \right].
\label{eq:app-bias-start}
\end{equation}
Fix $X=x$ and $T=\tau$. Substituting $v=(y'-y)/h_Y$ in the definition of the kernel average and
Taylor-expanding the conditional density in its second argument around $y$, using that $K_Y$
integrates to one, is symmetric (so $\int v K_Y(v)\dd v = 0$), and has finite second moment
$\kappa_{2,Y} := \int v^2 K_Y(v)\dd v$,
\[
\pE_{Y\mid T=\tau,X=x}[K_{h_Y}(Y-y)]
= \int K_Y(v)\,\pf{Y\mid T=\tau,X=x}(y+h_Yv)\dd v
= \pf{Y\mid T=\tau,X=x}(y) + \frac{h_Y^2}{2}\,\kappa_{2,Y}\,\partial_y^2 \pf{Y\mid T=\tau,X=x}(y)
+ o(h_Y^2),
\]
uniformly over $\tau$ in a neighbourhood of $t$ by the smoothness of Appendix~\ref{app:classical-regularity}.
Subtracting $\eta(x) = \pf{Y\mid T=t,X=x}(y)$ and writing $g(\tau) := \pf{Y\mid T=\tau,X=x}(y)$
for brevity,
\[
\pE_{Y\mid T=\tau,X=x}[K_{h_Y}(Y-y)] - \eta(x)
= \big( g(\tau) - g(t) \big) + \frac{h_Y^2}{2}\kappa_{2,Y}\,g''(\tau) + o(h_Y^2).
\]
Substitute $\tau = t + h_T\sigma$ in the outer, $T$-side average. By the same Taylor argument,
now in the first argument of $g$, $g(t+h_T\sigma) - g(t) = h_T\sigma\,g'(t) + \cO(h_T^2)$, and
$f_{T\mid X=x}(t+h_T\sigma) = \gps(t\mid x) + \cO(h_T)$. The inner average over $T\mid X=x$
becomes, after the substitution $\sigma = (\tau-t)/h_T$,
\[
\pE_{T\mid X=x}\Big[ K_{h_T}(T-t)\big(g(T)-\eta(x)\big) \Big]
= \int K_T(\sigma)\, f_{T\mid X=x}(t+h_T\sigma)
\Big[ h_T\sigma\,g'(t) + \frac{h_Y^2}{2}\kappa_{2,Y}\,g''(t) + \cO(h_T^2+h_Y^2) \Big] \dd\sigma .
\]
Expanding the product and integrating term by term: the leading piece
$\gps(t\mid x)\,h_T\,g'(t)\int\sigma K_T(\sigma)\dd\sigma$ vanishes because $K_T$ is symmetric,
which is exactly why a second-order kernel is required; the cross term between the $\cO(h_T)$
correction to $f_{T\mid X=x}$ and the $h_T\sigma g'(t)$ piece is $\cO(h_T^2)$ by the same
vanishing first moment; and the remaining terms collect into
\[
\pE_{T\mid X=x}\Big[ K_{h_T}(T-t)\big(g(T)-\eta(x)\big) \Big]
= \gps(t\mid x)\,\frac{h_Y^2}{2}\kappa_{2,Y}\,\partial_y^2\eta(x) + \cO(h_T^2+h_Y^2),
\]
using $g''(t) = \partial_y^2\eta(x)$. Substituting into~\eqref{eq:app-bias-start}, the factor
$\gps(t\mid x)$ cancels the $1/\gps(t\mid X)$ weight, and averaging the remainder over $X$
gives
\[
\pE_F\big[\IF_h(W;p_t(y),F)\big]
= \frac{h_Y^2}{2}\kappa_{2,Y}\,\pE_X\big[\partial_y^2\eta(X)\big] + \cO(h_T^2+h_Y^2)
= \cO(h_T^2+h_Y^2),
\]
which is the claimed bias. The symmetric, second-order kernel is used twice: to remove the
first-order term in $y$ (via $\int vK_Y=0$) and, independently, to remove the first-order term
in $t$ (via $\int\sigma K_T=0$); a first-order kernel would leave a bias of order $h_T$ or
$h_Y$, too slow for the undersmoothing conditions of Appendix~\ref{app:classical-regularity} to
control.

Two special cases recover known influence functions, and reproducing them checks the
derivation. If $T$ takes finitely many values, the point mass $\delta_{t_w}(t)$ is the
indicator $\Indi(t_w = t)$, no smoothing is needed in the treatment argument, and
$\gps(t \mid x)$ is the ordinary propensity score; \eqref{eq:app-if-smoothed} then reduces to
the influence function of \citet{kennedy2023density}. If the target is $\pE[Y(t)]$, the outcome
is integrated rather than evaluated: the derivation carries an extra factor $y$ and an extra
integral in $y$, the point mass $\delta_{y_w}(y)$ is replaced by $y_w$, and
\eqref{eq:app-if-smoothed} reduces to the influence function of \citet{kennedy2017continuous}.

\subsection{Second-order remainder}
\label{app:classical-remainder}

We prove Proposition~\ref{prop:dr-classical}. Let $\widehat\gps$ and $\pfhat{Y \mid T,X}$ be
nuisance estimates fitted on an independent fold, write $\eta(x) := \pf{Y \mid T=t,X=x}(y)$ and
$\widehat\eta(x) := \pfhat{Y \mid T=t,X=x}(y)$, and condition throughout on the fitting fold, so
that $\widehat\gps,\widehat\eta$ are fixed and the outer expectation $\pE$ below is over the
evaluation fold alone, at the true law $F$. Define the oracle functional, for any candidate pair
$(g,e)$,
\[
\theta(g,e) \;:=\; \pE\!\left[ \frac{K_{h_T}(T-t)}{e(t\mid X)}\big(K_{h_Y}(Y-y)-g(X)\big) + g(X) \right],
\]
so that the classical estimator satisfies $\pE\big[\widehat p^{\,\mathrm c}_t(y)\mid
\text{fitting fold}\big] = \theta(\widehat\eta,\widehat\gps)$, while
$\theta(\eta,\gps) = \pE_F\big[\IF_h(W;p_t(y),F)\big] + p_t(y)$ is the smoothed oracle
of Appendix~\ref{app:classical-kernels}, equal to $p_t(y) + \cO(h_T^2+h_Y^2)$. The remainder we
must bound is $\Rem_2 := \theta(\widehat\eta,\widehat\gps) - \theta(\eta,\gps) - \cO(h_T^2+h_Y^2)$,
and we compute the difference of the two oracles exactly.

Subtracting the two functionals and expanding the definition of $\theta$,
\begin{equation}
\theta(\widehat\eta,\widehat\gps) - \theta(\eta,\gps)
= \pE\!\left[ K_{h_T}(T-t)\left\{
\frac{K_{h_Y}(Y-y)-\widehat\eta(X)}{\widehat\gps(t\mid X)}
- \frac{K_{h_Y}(Y-y)-\eta(X)}{\gps(t\mid X)} \right\} \right]
+ \pE\big[\widehat\eta(X)-\eta(X)\big].
\label{eq:app-remainder-raw}
\end{equation}
Write $a := K_{h_Y}(Y-y)$, $e := \gps(t\mid X)$, $\widehat e := \widehat\gps(t\mid X)$ for
brevity. The identity
$(a-\widehat\eta)/\widehat e - (a-\eta)/e = (a-\eta)(1/\widehat e - 1/e) + (\eta-\widehat\eta)/\widehat e$,
obtained by adding and subtracting $\eta/\widehat e$, splits the bracket in~\eqref{eq:app-remainder-raw}
into two channels, so that~\eqref{eq:app-remainder-raw} becomes $T_1 + T_2 + T_3$ with
\begin{align}
T_1 &:= \pE\Big[ K_{h_T}(T-t)\big(K_{h_Y}(Y-y)-\eta(X)\big)\Big( \tfrac{1}{\widehat\gps(t\mid X)} - \tfrac{1}{\gps(t\mid X)} \Big) \Big], \label{eq:app-T1}\\
T_2 &:= \pE\Big[ K_{h_T}(T-t)\big(\eta(X)-\widehat\eta(X)\big)/\widehat\gps(t\mid X) \Big], \label{eq:app-T2}\\
T_3 &:= \pE\big[\widehat\eta(X)-\eta(X)\big]. \label{eq:app-T3}
\end{align}
Each channel is evaluated by conditioning on $X$, then on $T$, exactly as in
Appendix~\ref{app:classical-kernels}. For $T_2+T_3$, condition on $X$ and take the inner
expectation over $T\mid X$ first: $\pE_{T\mid X}[K_{h_T}(T-t)] = \gps(t\mid X) + \cO(h_T^2)$ by
the symmetric-kernel argument of Appendix~\ref{app:classical-kernels} applied to $\gps$ in place
of $\eta$, so
\[
T_2 + T_3
= \pE\Big[ \big(\eta(X)-\widehat\eta(X)\big)\Big(\tfrac{\gps(t\mid X)}{\widehat\gps(t\mid X)} - 1\Big) \Big] + \cO(h_T^2)
= \pE\!\left[ \big(\widehat\eta(X)-\eta(X)\big)\,\frac{\gps(t\mid X)-\widehat\gps(t\mid X)}{\widehat\gps(t\mid X)} \right] + \cO(h_T^2) .
\]
The $\cO(h_T^2)$ term is of smaller order than the product of nuisance errors under the rate
condition below, and is absorbed into the bias already controlled by
Appendix~\ref{app:classical-kernels}. For $T_1$, the same conditioning shows the bracket
$K_{h_Y}(Y-y)-\eta(X)$ has conditional mean, given $(T,X)$, equal to
$f_{Y\mid T,X}(y)-\eta(X) + \cO(h_Y^2)$, which is itself $\cO(|T-t|)$ inside the effective
support of $K_{h_T}(T-t)$ by smoothness of $f_{Y\mid T,X}(y)$ in $t$
(Appendix~\ref{app:classical-regularity}); hence $T_1 = \cO(h_T+h_Y^2)$ times a nuisance-error
average, one order smaller in $h$ than $T_2+T_3$, and negligible relative to it. Collecting the
dominant channel gives
\begin{equation}
\Rem_2
= \pE\!\left[
\left( \frac{K_{h_T}(T-t)}{\widehat\gps(t \mid X)}
- \frac{K_{h_T}(T-t)}{\gps(t \mid X)} \right)
\Big( \eta(X) - \widehat\eta(X) \Big)
\right] + \cO(h_T^2),
\label{eq:app-remainder}
\end{equation}
recovering the cross term claimed above, up to the kernel-bias term already accounted for. This
is a product of the two nuisance errors: the first factor is the error in the inverse propensity
weight and the second is the error in the conditional density. Applying the Cauchy--Schwarz
inequality and bounding $\widehat\gps^{-1}$ and $\gps^{-1}$ using the lower bound $\gps_{\min}$
of Assumption~\ref{as:identification}(iii) and the boundedness of $K_{h_T}$,
\[
|\Rem_2| \;\lesssim\; \big\| \widehat\gps - \gps \big\|_{L_2}\,
\big\| \widehat\eta - \eta \big\|_{L_2} + \cO(h_T^2) .
\]
The remainder is therefore controlled by the product of the two $L_2$ errors, plus a
kernel-bias term already of order $\cO(h_T^2)$ under Appendix~\ref{app:classical-regularity}. It
is $o(n^{-1/2})$ whenever each nuisance error is $o(n^{-1/4})$, and one error may be larger if
the other is correspondingly smaller, so that the product still vanishes faster than
$n^{-1/2}$; this is exactly the double robustness asserted in
Proposition~\ref{prop:dr-classical}. \hfill$\square$

\section{The contrastive correction}
\label{app:mcd}

This appendix proves the results of Section~\ref{sec:mcd-form}. We first prove the
reconstruction identity, then differentiate the contrastive factorization to obtain
Proposition~\ref{prop:if-mcd}.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:reconstruction}}
\label{app:mcd-reconstruction}

From Definition~\ref{def:MCF}, the odds of the contrast function are the ratio of its two
mixture components,
\[
\frac{\contrast}{1-\contrast}
= \frac{\ratio\,\pf{T,X,Y}(t,x,y)}{(1-\ratio)\,\pf{T,X}(t,x)\,\pf{Y}(y)}
= \frac{\ratio}{1-\ratio}\,\frac{\pf{Y \mid T=t,X=x}(y)}{\pf{Y}(y)} ,
\]
where the second equality uses the factorization
$\pf{T,X,Y}(t,x,y) = \pf{T,X}(t,x)\,\pf{Y \mid T=t,X=x}(y)$ to cancel $\pf{T,X}(t,x)$.
Multiplying both sides by $(1-\ratio)/\ratio$ isolates
$\frac{1-\ratio}{\ratio}\,\contrast/(1-\contrast) = \pf{Y \mid T=t,X=x}(y)/\pf{Y}(y)$, and by
the definition of $\DensityRatio$ in~\eqref{eq:reformulation} the left-hand side is
$\DensityRatio(t,x,y)$. Rearranging gives both
$\DensityRatio = \frac{1-\ratio}{\ratio}\,\contrast/(1-\contrast)$ and
$\pf{Y \mid T=t,X=x}(y) = \pf{Y}(y)\,\DensityRatio$, which are the two identities
of~\eqref{eq:reconstruction}. \hfill$\square$

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:if-mcd}}
\label{app:mcd-derivation}

We differentiate the contrastive factorization~\eqref{eq:factorization},
$p_t(y) = \pf{Y}(y)\,m(t,y)$. By the product rule, Lemma~\ref{lem:product},
\begin{equation}
\IF\big(w;p_t(y),F\big)
= \pf{Y}(y)\,\IF\big(w;m(t,y),F\big)
+ m(t,y)\,\IF\big(w;\pf{Y}(y),F\big).
\label{eq:app-product}
\end{equation}
We compute the two influence functions on the right in turn.

For the marginal factor, $\pf{Y}(y)$ depends on $F$ only through the marginal law of the
outcome, evaluated at the fixed point $y$. This is the univariate special case of the
computation in Appendix~\ref{app:classical-derivative}: differentiating a marginal density at a
fixed point along the contamination path returns the difference between a point mass and the
density,
\begin{equation}
\IF\big(w;\pf{Y}(y),F\big) = \delta_{y_w}(y) - \pf{Y}(y),
\label{eq:app-if-fY}
\end{equation}
which is regularized by $\delta_{y_w}(y) \rightsquigarrow K_{h_Y}(y_w - y)$ as in
Appendix~\ref{app:classical-kernels}.

For the covariate-averaged factor $m(t,y) = \pE_X[\DensityRatio(t,X,y)]$, the integrand
$\DensityRatio(t,X,y;F)$ depends on $F$ both through the law of $X$ and through its own
functional form, since the density ratio is itself a functional of $F$. Lemma~\ref{lem:if-expectation}
is written for exactly this situation, and gives
\begin{equation}
\IF\big(w;m(t,y),F\big)
= \DensityRatio(t,x_w,y) - m(t,y)
+ \pE_X\big[ \IF(w;\DensityRatio(t,X,y),F) \big].
\label{eq:app-if-m}
\end{equation}
The third term is present precisely because the density ratio is fitted rather than known; it
is the term that will carry the influence function of the classifier. Substituting the odds
rule~\eqref{eq:app-if-R} of Lemma~\ref{lem:odds} for $\IF(w;\DensityRatio,F)$ inside that
expectation turns it into
$\frac{1-\ratio}{\ratio}\,\pE_X[\IF(w;\contrast,F)/(1-\contrast)^2]$.

Finally, substitute the marginal influence function~\eqref{eq:app-if-fY} and the covariate
influence function~\eqref{eq:app-if-m}, with the odds rule applied, into the
product~\eqref{eq:app-product}:
\[
\IF\big(w;p_t(y),F\big)
= \pf{Y}(y)\left[ \DensityRatio(t,x_w,y) - m(t,y)
+ \frac{1-\ratio}{\ratio}\,\pE_X\!\left[\frac{\IF(w;\contrast,F)}{(1-\contrast)^2}\right] \right]
+ m(t,y)\Big(\delta_{y_w}(y) - \pf{Y}(y)\Big).
\]
Grouping the first three terms, all multiplied by $\pf{Y}(y)$, inside a single brace and leaving
the last term, carried by $m(t,y)\,\IF(w;\pf{Y}(y),F)$, on its own is exactly~\eqref{eq:if-mcd},
the claim of Proposition~\ref{prop:if-mcd}. \hfill$\square$

\section{The differentiable discriminant}
\label{app:logistic}

This appendix proves the results of Section~\ref{sec:mcd-general-classifier} and the
specialization of Section~\ref{sec:mcd-logistic}.
Appendix~\ref{app:logistic-grads} records the gradients, the Hessian and the convexity, and
proves Proposition~\ref{prop:logistic-regularity}.
Appendix~\ref{app:logistic-channels} differentiates the two component laws.
Appendix~\ref{app:logistic-ift} assembles these into Theorem~\ref{thm:channels}.
Appendices~\ref{app:logistic-centering} and~\ref{app:logistic-zero-mean} prove the closed form
(Proposition~\ref{prop:closed-form}) and the zero-mean property (Lemma~\ref{lem:zero-mean}), and
Appendix~\ref{app:logistic-collapse} proves Corollary~\ref{cor:collapse}. Throughout,
$\beta = \beta_\lambda(F)$, $D := D_\beta$, and all expectations are taken at $F$.

One convention keeps the displays readable. We write $\phi := \phi(t,x,y)$ for the logit
Jacobian $J_\beta$ throughout. Only Appendix~\ref{app:logistic-grads} uses that it is a
\emph{fixed} map, through $\nabla^2_\beta F_\beta \equiv 0$; that subsection is therefore the
one place where the argument is specific to the affine logit of
Section~\ref{sec:mcd-logistic}, and it is exactly where
Proposition~\ref{prop:logistic-regularity} is proved. No other derivation below ever
differentiates $\phi$, so every one of them holds verbatim with $\phi$ read as
$J_\beta$, which is what makes the results of Section~\ref{sec:mcd-general-classifier} general
and Section~\ref{sec:mcd-mlp} short.

\subsection{Gradients, Hessian and convexity}
\label{app:logistic-grads}

Differentiating the logistic loss in its first argument,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial D}(D,z)
= -\frac{z}{D} + \frac{1-z}{1-D}
= \frac{D-z}{D(1-D)} .
\label{eq:app-dl}
\end{equation}
Differentiating the model in $\beta$, with $\sigma' = \sigma(1-\sigma)$,
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\beta D_\beta = D(1-D)\,\phi .
\label{eq:app-grad-D}
\end{equation}
Composing the two by the chain rule, the factor $D(1-D)$ cancels and the loss gradient
simplifies to
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\beta \ell(D_\beta,z) = (D-z)\,\phi ,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
u_\beta = (D-1)\phi, \quad v_\beta = D\phi ,
\label{eq:app-uv}
\end{equation}
where $u_\beta$ and $v_\beta$ are the gradients at labels $z=1$ and $z=0$. Differentiating once
more and using~\eqref{eq:app-grad-D}, both labels give the same second derivative
$\nabla_\beta u_\beta = \nabla_\beta v_\beta = D(1-D)\phi\phi^\top$, so the Hessian of the
penalized risk is
\begin{equation}
H_\lambda
= \pE_{P_\ratio}\big[ D(1-D)\,\phi\phi^\top \big] + 2\lambda I_{\ks} .
\label{eq:app-hessian}
\end{equation}
For any $s \in \R^{\ks}$,
\[
s^\top H_\lambda s
= \pE_{P_\ratio}\big[ D(1-D)(s^\top\phi)^2 \big] + 2\lambda\|s\|^2
\;\ge\; 2\lambda\|s\|^2 ,
\]
since $D(1-D) > 0$ makes the first term nonnegative. Hence $H_\lambda$ is positive definite with
every eigenvalue at least $2\lambda$, the criterion~\eqref{eq:criterion} is strictly convex, and
$\beta_\lambda(F)$ exists, is unique and is interior. When $\lambda = 0$ the same computation
gives positive definiteness provided $\pE_{P_\ratio}[(s^\top\phi)^2] > 0$ for every $s \neq 0$,
i.e.\ provided $\phi$ is not collinear on the support. The first-order condition
$\nabla_\beta \cR_\lambda = 0$ reads, using~\eqref{eq:app-uv},
\begin{equation}
G_\lambda(\beta;F)
:= \ratio\,\pE_{P_1}[u_\beta] + (1-\ratio)\,\pE_{P_0}[v_\beta] + 2\lambda\beta = 0 .
\label{eq:app-foc}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Perturbation of the two component laws}
\label{app:logistic-channels}

We differentiate each component of the mixture $P_\ratio$ along the contamination path,
treating $\beta$ as fixed; the dependence of $\beta$ on $F$ is restored in
Appendix~\ref{app:logistic-ift}.

The positive law is the observed law, $P_1(F_\varepsilon) = F_\varepsilon$, so
$\pE_{F_\varepsilon}[u] = (1-\varepsilon)\pE_F[u] + \varepsilon\,u(w)$ is affine in
$\varepsilon$, and its derivative is
\begin{equation}
\partial_\varepsilon\, \pE_{P_1(F_\varepsilon)}[u]\big|_{0}
= u(w) - \pE_{P_1}[u].
\label{eq:app-channel1}
\end{equation}

The negative law is the product $P_0(F_\varepsilon) = F_{T,X,\varepsilon} \otimes F_{Y,\varepsilon}$,
whose two factors are the contaminated marginals of $F_\varepsilon$,
\[
F_{T,X,\varepsilon} = (1-\varepsilon)F_{T,X} + \varepsilon\,\delta_{(t_w,x_w)},
\qquad
F_{Y,\varepsilon} = (1-\varepsilon)F_Y + \varepsilon\,\delta_{y_w} .
\]
Expanding the double integral $\pE_{P_0(F_\varepsilon)}[v]$ into the four terms according to
which factor carries the contamination,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\pE_{P_0(F_\varepsilon)}[v]
&= (1-\varepsilon)^2\,\pE_{P_0}[v]
+ (1-\varepsilon)\varepsilon\,\pE_{F_{T,X}}\big[ v(T,X,y_w) \big] \\
&\quad + \varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)\,\pE_{F_Y}\big[ v(t_w,x_w,\widetilde Y) \big]
+ \varepsilon^2\, v(t_w,x_w,y_w).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:app-p0-expanded}
\end{equation}
Differentiate at $\varepsilon = 0$. The quadratic term $\varepsilon^2 v(t_w,x_w,y_w)$ has zero
derivative; the factor $(1-\varepsilon)^2$ contributes $-2\,\pE_{P_0}[v]$; and each of the two
cross terms, being $\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)$ times a constant, contributes that constant.
Hence
\begin{equation}
\partial_\varepsilon\, \pE_{P_0(F_\varepsilon)}[v]\big|_{0}
= \pE_{F_Y}\big[ v(t_w,x_w,\widetilde Y) \big]
+ \pE_{F_{T,X}}\big[ v(T,X,y_w) \big]
- 2\,\pE_{P_0}[v].
\label{eq:app-channel0}
\end{equation}
The coefficient $-2$ on the centring term is what splits the contrastive derivative into two
channels, one for each factor of the product measure. Equivalently, applying the product rule
of Lemma~\ref{lem:product} to the bilinear map
$(\mu,\nu) \mapsto \iint v \dd\mu \dd\nu$ produces the first bracket by differentiating
$F_{T,X,\varepsilon}$ at fixed $F_Y$ and the second by differentiating $F_{Y,\varepsilon}$ at
fixed $F_{T,X}$. The mixture ratio $\ratio$ is a design constant and contributes no derivative.

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:channels}}
\label{app:logistic-ift}

By definition the perturbed minimizer $\beta_\lambda(F_\varepsilon)$ solves the first-order
condition $G_\lambda\big(\beta_\lambda(F_\varepsilon);F_\varepsilon\big) = 0$ identically in
$\varepsilon$. The left-hand side depends on $\varepsilon$ in two ways, through the parameter
$\beta_\lambda(F_\varepsilon)$ and through the law $F_\varepsilon$. Differentiating the identity
at $\varepsilon = 0$ by the implicit function theorem,
\begin{equation}
\underbrace{\nabla_\beta G_\lambda}_{=\,H_\lambda}\,
\underbrace{\partial_\varepsilon \beta_\lambda(F_\varepsilon)\big|_{0}}_{=\,\IF(w;\beta_\lambda,F)}
+ \underbrace{\partial_\varepsilon G_\lambda(\beta;F_\varepsilon)\big|_{0}}_{=:\,\score(w;\beta)}
= 0 .
\label{eq:app-ift}
\end{equation}
The first factor is $\nabla_\beta G_\lambda = H_\lambda$ by~\eqref{eq:app-hessian}, and it is
invertible by Assumption~\ref{as:regularity}, which Appendix~\ref{app:logistic-grads}
establishes outright in the affine case; solving gives
$\IF(w;\beta_\lambda,F) = -H_\lambda^{-1}\score(w;\beta)$, which is the first equation
of~\eqref{eq:master}. It remains to identify $\score = \partial_\varepsilon G_\lambda|_0$.
Since $G_\lambda(\beta;F_\varepsilon)
= \ratio\,\pE_{P_1(F_\varepsilon)}[u_\beta] + (1-\ratio)\,\pE_{P_0(F_\varepsilon)}[v_\beta]
+ 2\lambda\beta$ and the penalty $2\lambda\beta$ does not depend on $F$, applying
\eqref{eq:app-channel1} with $u = u_\beta$ to the first expectation and
\eqref{eq:app-channel0} with $v = v_\beta$ to the second gives the three summands
\[
\score
= \ratio\big(u_\beta(w) - \pE_{P_1}[u_\beta]\big)
+ (1-\ratio)\Big(\pE_{F_Y}[v_\beta(t_w,x_w,\widetilde Y)] - \pE_{P_0}[v_\beta]\Big)
+ (1-\ratio)\Big(\pE_{F_{T,X}}[v_\beta(T,X,y_w)] - \pE_{P_0}[v_\beta]\Big),
\]
which are exactly $\score_1$, $\score_0^{TX}$ and $\score_0^{Y}$ of~\eqref{eq:channels}. This
establishes both equations of~\eqref{eq:master} and hence Theorem~\ref{thm:channels}. \hfill$\square$

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:closed-form}}
\label{app:logistic-centering}

We evaluate the three channels for the logistic model. By~\eqref{eq:app-uv}, $v_\beta = D\phi$,
so every centring term $\pE_{P_0}[v_\beta]$ equals $\pE_{P_0}[D\phi] = b_0$, and the two
contaminated contrastive terms are $b_{TX}(t_w,x_w)$ and $b_Y(y_w)$ of~\eqref{eq:b-channels}.
Summing the three channels of~\eqref{eq:channels} and grouping the two centring terms with the
observed centring term,
\begin{equation}
\score(w;\beta)
= \ratio\,u_\beta(w) + (1-\ratio)\big( b_{TX}(t_w,x_w) + b_Y(y_w) \big)
- \Big( \ratio\,\pE_{P_1}[u_\beta] + 2(1-\ratio)\,b_0 \Big).
\label{eq:app-S-raw}
\end{equation}
The bracketed quantity contains the unknown expectation $\pE_{P_1}[u_\beta]$, which the
first-order condition~\eqref{eq:app-foc} pins down: rearranging
$\ratio\,\pE_{P_1}[u_\beta] + (1-\ratio)b_0 + 2\lambda\beta_\lambda = 0$ gives
$\ratio\,\pE_{P_1}[u_\beta] = -2\lambda\beta_\lambda - (1-\ratio)b_0$. Substituting this into the
bracket,
\begin{equation}
-\Big( \ratio\,\pE_{P_1}[u_\beta] + 2(1-\ratio)b_0 \Big)
= 2\lambda\beta_\lambda + (1-\ratio)b_0 - 2(1-\ratio)b_0
= 2\lambda\beta_\lambda - (1-\ratio)b_0 .
\label{eq:app-resolve}
\end{equation}
The uncancelled residual $-(1-\ratio)b_0$ appears because the first-order condition supplies a
single contrastive centring term whereas the channels of Theorem~\ref{thm:channels} produce
two. Combining~\eqref{eq:app-S-raw} and~\eqref{eq:app-resolve} and writing
$u_\beta = (D-1)\phi$ from~\eqref{eq:app-uv} gives
\[
\score(w;\beta)
= \ratio\big(D_\beta(w)-1\big)\phi(w)
+ (1-\ratio)\big(b_{TX}(t_w,x_w) + b_Y(y_w) - b_0\big)
+ 2\lambda\beta_\lambda,
\]
which is exactly~\eqref{eq:S}, the claim of Proposition~\ref{prop:closed-form}. \hfill$\square$

Two limiting cases are worth recording, as they connect the formula to familiar ones. Setting
$\lambda = 0$ removes the penalty term $2\lambda\beta_\lambda$. Setting in addition the two
contrastive channels to zero, the situation of a negative class drawn from a distribution
fixed by the analyst, so that $b_{TX}$, $b_Y$ and $b_0$ carry no dependence on $F$, leaves
$\score(w;\beta) = \ratio\big(D_\beta(w)-1\big)\phi(w)$, and~\eqref{eq:master} becomes
$-H_0^{-1}\big(D_\beta(w) - z_w\big)\phi(w)$, the classical influence function of logistic
regression.

\subsection{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:zero-mean}}
\label{app:logistic-zero-mean}

We take the expectation of the closed form~\eqref{eq:S} over $W \sim F$. The key structural
fact is that when $W = (T,X,Y)$ is drawn from $F$, its first two components jointly follow
$F_{T,X}$ and its last component follows $F_Y$, the same marginals that define $P_0$.

The first term is an expectation under $P_1 = F_{T,X,Y}$, so it equals
$\ratio\,\pE_{P_1}[(D-1)\phi] = \ratio\,\pE_{P_1}[u_\beta]$ by~\eqref{eq:app-uv}.

The vector $b_{TX}(T,X) = \pE_{\widetilde Y \sim F_Y}[v_\beta(T,X,\widetilde Y)]$ is a function
of $(T,X)$ alone. Averaging it over $(T,X) \sim F_{T,X}$ and using Fubini together with the
product structure of $P_0$,
\[
\pE\big[ b_{TX}(T,X) \big]
= \pE_{(T,X) \sim F_{T,X}} \pE_{\widetilde Y \sim F_Y}\big[ v_\beta(T,X,\widetilde Y) \big]
= \pE_{P_0}[v_\beta] = b_0 .
\]
Symmetrically, $b_Y(Y) = \pE_{(T,X) \sim F_{T,X}}[v_\beta(T,X,Y)]$ is a function of $Y$ alone
and, averaged over $Y \sim F_Y$, also equals $b_0$.

The vectors $b_0$ and $2\lambda\beta_\lambda$ are constants and average to themselves. Summing
the four contributions,
\[
\pE\big[ \score(W;\beta) \big]
= \ratio\,\pE_{P_1}[u_\beta] + (1-\ratio)\big( b_0 + b_0 - b_0 \big) + 2\lambda\beta_\lambda
= \ratio\,\pE_{P_1}[u_\beta] + (1-\ratio)b_0 + 2\lambda\beta_\lambda,
\]
which is exactly $G_\lambda(\beta_\lambda;F)$ and therefore vanishes by the first-order
condition~\eqref{eq:app-foc}. This proves $\pE_{W\sim F}[\score(W;\beta_\lambda)] = 0$. Since
$H_\lambda$ does not depend on $w$, taking the expectation of
$\IF(w;\beta_\lambda,F) = -H_\lambda^{-1}\score(w;\beta_\lambda)$ gives
$\pE_{W\sim F}[\IF(W;\beta_\lambda,F)] = -H_\lambda^{-1}\pE[\score] = 0$, which is the second
statement of Lemma~\ref{lem:zero-mean}. \hfill$\square$

The computation also shows what would go wrong without the residual: had the term
$-(1-\ratio)b_0$ been omitted from~\eqref{eq:S}, the contrastive contributions would have summed
to $2b_0$ instead of $b_0$, and the total mean would have been $(1-\ratio)b_0 \neq 0$, which is
impossible for an influence function.

\subsection{Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:collapse}}
\label{app:logistic-collapse}

We collapse the correction term to an inner product. By~\eqref{eq:if-chain} together with the
model gradient~\eqref{eq:app-grad-D},
\[
\IF\big(w;\contrast(t,x,y),F\big)
= \nabla_\beta D_\beta(t,x,y)^\top \IF(w;\beta_\lambda,F)
= D(1-D)\,\phi(t,x,y)^\top\,\IF\big(w;\beta_\lambda,F\big).
\]
Divide by $(1-D)^2$, multiply by $(1-\ratio)/\ratio$, and take the expectation over the
covariates. Because $\IF(w;\beta_\lambda,F)$ does not depend on $X$, it factors out of the
expectation, and the scalar weight simplifies to the density ratio,
\[
\frac{1-\ratio}{\ratio}\,\frac{D(1-D)}{(1-D)^2}
= \frac{1-\ratio}{\ratio}\,\frac{D}{1-D}
= \DensityRatio ,
\]
by the definition
$\DensityRatio_\beta := \tfrac{1-\ratio}{\ratio}\,D_\beta/(1-D_\beta)$. Hence
\[
\frac{1-\ratio}{\ratio}\,
\pE_X\!\left[ \frac{\IF(w;\contrast(t,X,y),F)}{(1-\contrast(t,X,y))^2} \right]
= \pE_X\big[ \DensityRatio(t,X,y)\,\phi(t,X,y) \big]^\top \IF(w;\beta_\lambda,F)
= a_\lambda(t,y)^\top \IF(w;\beta_\lambda,F),
\]
which is~\eqref{eq:collapse}, the claim of Corollary~\ref{cor:collapse}. \hfill$\square$

Substituting $\IF(w;\beta_\lambda,F) = -H_\lambda^{-1}\score(w;\beta)$ and using the symmetry of
$H_\lambda$, with $\xi(t,y) := H_\lambda^{-1}a_\lambda(t,y)$,
\[
a_\lambda(t,y)^\top \IF\big(w;\beta_\lambda,F\big)
= -\,\big( H_\lambda^{-1}a_\lambda(t,y) \big)^\top \score(w;\beta)
= -\,\xi(t,y)^\top \score(w;\beta),
\]
which is the form used in the estimator~\eqref{eq:estimator} and requires one linear solve per
evaluation point rather than one per observation.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:normalization}}
\label{app:asymptotics-normalization}

Integrating the joint density over $(t,x)$ recovers the marginal of the outcome, and factoring
through the conditional density,
\[
\pf{Y}(y)
= \iint \pf{T,X,Y}(t,x,y) \dd t \dd x
= \iint \pf{T,X}(t,x)\,\pf{Y \mid T=t,X=x}(y) \dd t \dd x .
\]
Substituting the reconstruction identity~\eqref{eq:reconstruction},
$\pf{Y \mid T=t,X=x}(y) = \pf{Y}(y)\,\DensityRatio(t,x,y)$, and taking the constant $\pf{Y}(y)$
outside the integral,
\[
\pf{Y}(y)
= \pf{Y}(y) \iint \pf{T,X}(t,x)\,\DensityRatio(t,x,y) \dd t \dd x
= \pf{Y}(y)\,\pE_{(T,X)}\big[ \DensityRatio(T,X,y) \big].
\]
Since $\pf{Y}(y) > 0$, dividing both sides by it gives
$\pE_{(T,X)}[\DensityRatio(T,X,y)] = 1$, which is~\eqref{eq:normalization}, the claim of
Proposition~\ref{prop:normalization}. \hfill$\square$

Written in terms of the contrast function, the identity reads
$\pE_{(T,X)}\big[\contrast(T,X,y)/(1-\contrast(T,X,y))\big] = \ratio/(1-\ratio)$ for every such
$y$. It holds for each $y$ separately and therefore constitutes a continuum of restrictions
linking the contrast function to the marginal density of the outcome, which is the sense in
which the two factors of the correction are not variationally independent.

\section{Data-generating processes}
\label{app:dgp}

This appendix gives the full specification of every data-generating process used in
Section~\ref{sec:simulation}, referenced there only by name.

\subsection{The AXY dataset}
\label{app:dgp-axy}

AXY is a binary-treatment, high-dimensional-covariate design, adapted from the toy structural
model of \citet{melnychuk2023flows} (their Figure~1 and Appendix~H.1): its two treatment arms
and their quadratic-versus-linear asymmetry are theirs, generalized here from a scalar
covariate to a projection of a $\mathrm{cov\_dim}$-dimensional one, so that the covariate
dimension can be swept while the treatment stays binary:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
X &\sim 0.5\,\mathcal N(0,I_{\mathrm{cov\_dim}}) + 0.5\,\mathcal N(\mathrm{cov\_shift}\cdot w, I_{\mathrm{cov\_dim}}),
\qquad w := \mathrm{cov\_dim}^{-1/2}\mathbf{1}, \\
\gps(1\mid x) &= \frac{\mathcal N(x;0,I)}{\mathcal N(x;0,I)+\mathcal N(x;\mathrm{cov\_shift}\cdot w,I)},
\qquad T\mid X=x \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}\big(\gps(1\mid x)\big), \\
Y &= U_Y + \begin{cases} Z^2 - 1.82\,Z + 2 & \text{if } T=1 \\ 2.18\,Z + 1.5 & \text{if } T=0 \end{cases},
\qquad Z := w^\top X,\ \ U_Y\sim\mathcal N(0,1),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:app-axy}
\end{equation}
with $\gps(\cdot\mid x)$ available in closed form by construction, the one property this design
is used for in Section~\ref{sec:sim-experiments}: it lets the classical
estimator~\eqref{eq:est-classical} be computed directly, without a further nuisance-estimation
step, and checked against the IF-corrected one~\eqref{eq:estimator} up to sampling error.
$\mathrm{cov\_shift}$ controls the overlap between the two covariate sub-populations, exactly as
$b$ does in \citet{melnychuk2023flows}'s original design: small values give near-complete
overlap, large values push $\gps(1\mid x)$ towards $0$ or $1$.

$w$ is the unit vector along the shift between the two mixture components, so $Z=w^\top X$ is a
one-dimensional sufficient statistic for the treatment mechanism, and its marginal law is exactly
$0.5\,\mathcal N(0,1) + 0.5\,\mathcal N(\mathrm{cov\_shift},1)$: \emph{independent of
$\mathrm{cov\_dim}$}, since $w^\top w = 1$ by construction. Consequently $p_t(y)$
(Figure~\ref{fig:dgp-toyscm-axy}) does not change shape as $\mathrm{cov\_dim}$ grows: growing the
covariate dimension changes nothing about the \emph{target}, only about how hard $\gps$ and the
conditional density are to estimate from a finite sample, which is precisely why this design
isolates the effect of confounder dimensionality (Section~\ref{sec:discussion-simpler}) from the
effect of a harder target.

\subsection{The well-specified linear-Gaussian model}
\label{app:dgp-wellspecified}

Every design above and below is deliberately misspecified for a low-degree polynomial feature
map: AXY's covariate is a Gaussian mixture, and each of the four models of
Appendix~\ref{app:dgp-continuous} adds a property (multimodality, heavy tails, heteroscedasticity,
skewness) that a Gaussian log-density cannot produce. This leaves open a question none of them can
answer: does the estimator behave correctly when nothing is fighting the feature map, only sampling
noise? A confounder $Z\sim\mathcal N(\mu_Z,\Sigma_Z)\in\R^{\mathrm{cov\_dim}}$ and a linear-Gaussian
outcome answer it,
\begin{equation}
Y \mid T=t,Z=z \ \sim\ \mathcal N\big(a^\top z + b^\top t,\ \sigma^2\big),
\label{eq:app-dgp-wellspec}
\end{equation}
with $T$ continuous and of any dimension in this design's native form, matching the treatment this
paper targets; the runs of Section~\ref{sec:sim-experiments} constrain it to a binary $T$ only
because the baselines of Appendix~\ref{app:glossary} it is compared against there require a
discrete treatment; the design itself supports a continuous one.
Since $\mathrm{do}(T=t)$ severs $Z\to T$, $Z$ reverts to its own marginal exactly as in
Appendix~\ref{app:dgp-continuous}, giving
\begin{equation}
Y \mid \mathrm{do}(T=t) \ \sim\ \mathcal N\big(a^\top\mu_Z + b^\top t,\ a^\top\Sigma_Z a + \sigma^2\big),
\label{eq:app-dgp-wellspec-density}
\end{equation}
exactly, with no quadrature or Monte Carlo step anywhere in the computation, a stronger sense of
closed form than any other design here, since even the elementary skew-normal case needs the
adjusted-parameter substitution of~\eqref{eq:app-dgp-skew-density} and AXY's quadratic arm needs a
numerical integral over its mixture confounder.

This is also the only design in which the contrast function's log-odds is an \emph{exact} low-degree
polynomial. Both $f(y\mid t,z)$ and, because $Z$ and $T$ are jointly Gaussian under the sampling
law, the marginal $f_Y(y)$ are Gaussian densities, so $\log f(y\mid t,z) - \log f_Y(y)$ is exactly a
quadratic form in $(t,z,y)$: every term is degree two or less, with no exponential, mixture, or
Gamma-mixture structure left over the way there is in every other design above. A degree-two
polynomial feature map (Table~\ref{tab:cv-grids}'s feature-map-degree grid) therefore represents it
without approximation error, so that $D_{\beta_\lambda} = \contrast$ exactly rather than only in the
limit. This is precisely the condition under which nothing is left to control on the
approximation side: this design isolates the estimation-only regime, the one AXY and the four
models of Appendix~\ref{app:dgp-continuous} cannot exercise on their own, since a low-degree
polynomial is misspecified for all of them.

\subsection{The four continuous, multidimensional-treatment models}
\label{app:dgp-continuous}

All four models below share the same confounding structure: $(X,T)$ jointly Gaussian,
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix} X \\ T \end{pmatrix} \sim
\mathcal{N}\!\left( \begin{pmatrix} \mu_X \\ \mu_T \end{pmatrix},
\begin{pmatrix} \Sigma_X & \Sigma_{XT} \\ \Sigma_{XT}^\top & \Sigma_T \end{pmatrix} \right),
\qquad X \in \R^{p}, \ T \in \R^{d},
\label{eq:app-confounding}
\end{equation}
with $p=\dim(X)$ and $d=\dim(T)$ both free to vary, and $\Sigma_{XT}$ controlling the strength of
confounding. Since $\mathrm{do}(T=t)$ severs $X \to T$, $X$ reverts to its own marginal
$\mathcal N(\mu_X,\Sigma_X)$ under the intervention regardless of $\Sigma_{XT}$; every closed form
below uses only this marginal, never the conditional law of $X$ given $T$. What varies across the
four models is only the outcome law $Y \mid T,X$, since, as already stressed in
Section~\ref{sec:sim-dgp}, that is the only part of the model that can change the shape of
$p_t(y)$.

Each of the four adds exactly one feature a jointly Gaussian $(X,T,Y)$ cannot produce, and each
feature is chosen because a purely Gaussian, linear-in-$(t,x)$ specification of $Y\mid T,X$,
the assumption underlying a plug-in built on a conditional mean and a constant-variance noise
model, represents it either not at all or only by contorting the mean and variance to
compensate. Together the four probe whether the discriminant can recover a shape a mean-and-variance
description would miss, before Section~\ref{sec:sim-experiments} asks the sharper question of
\emph{which} discriminant, and at what cost.

\paragraph{Multimodal.} A two-component mixture whose \emph{weight}, not only its component means,
depends on $t$:
\begin{equation}
Y \mid T=t,X=x \ \sim\ w(t)\,\mathcal N(a_1^\top x + b_1^\top t,\, s_1^2)
+ (1-w(t))\,\mathcal N(a_2^\top x + b_2^\top t,\, s_2^2), \qquad
w(t) = \sigma(c_0 + c_1^\top t).
\label{eq:app-dgp-mixture}
\end{equation}
Because $w(t)$ does not depend on $x$, integrating $x$ against its own marginal acts separately on
each mixture component:
\begin{equation}
p_t(y) = w(t)\,\mathcal N\big(y;\, a_1^\top\mu_X + b_1^\top t,\, a_1^\top\Sigma_X a_1 + s_1^2\big)
+ (1-w(t))\,\mathcal N\big(y;\, a_2^\top\mu_X + b_2^\top t,\, a_2^\top\Sigma_X a_2 + s_2^2\big).
\label{eq:app-dgp-mixture-density}
\end{equation}
The difficulty parameters are the separation between the two components ($\|b_1-b_2\|$,
$\|a_1-a_2\|$) and $c_1$, how quickly the mixing weight moves with $t$: both jointly control how
easy the resulting bimodality is to detect at a given sample size. A weight that moves with $t$
makes $p_t(y)$ change \emph{shape}, not only location or scale, as $t$ varies, exactly the kind
of dependence on $t$ that a discriminant with too little capacity in the treatment coordinate
cannot represent, which is what varying $c_1$ at fixed $n$ is built to expose
(Section~\ref{sec:sim-experiments}).

\paragraph{Heavy-tailed.} A linear-Gaussian mean with a standardized Student-$t$ noise term in
place of a Gaussian one:
\begin{equation}
Y \mid T=t,X=x \;=\; a^\top x + b^\top t + \sigma\,\varepsilon, \qquad
\varepsilon \sim \text{standardized Student-}t(\nu).
\label{eq:app-dgp-heavytail}
\end{equation}
$p_t(y)$ is now the convolution of a Gaussian ($a^\top X$, $X\sim\mathcal N(\mu_X,\Sigma_X)$) with
a scaled Student-$t$, which has no elementary closed form, but does have an exact semi-closed form
through the Student-$t$'s Gamma precision-mixture representation
($\varepsilon\mid W \sim \mathcal N(0,c_\nu/W)$, $W \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\nu/2,\nu/2)$, mean $1$,
$c_\nu = (\nu-2)/\nu$ for $\nu>2$ the factor that standardizes $\varepsilon$ to unit variance):
\begin{equation}
p_t(y) = \pE_W\Big[\, \mathcal N\big(y;\, a^\top\mu_X + b^\top t,\ a^\top\Sigma_X a + \sigma^2
c_\nu / W \big) \,\Big], \qquad W \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\nu/2,\nu/2).
\label{eq:app-dgp-heavytail-density}
\end{equation}
The one remaining integral, over $W$ alone, is evaluated by a deterministic quadrature at $400$
equally spaced quantiles of the $\mathrm{Gamma}(\nu/2,\nu/2)$ law, not by Monte Carlo: the result
is exact to machine precision and identical across repeated evaluations, which is what makes it
usable as ground truth. Smaller $\nu$ gives heavier tails; $\nu\to\infty$ recovers the plain
Gaussian, linear model as a continuity check. A heavy tail is a property of $p_t(y)$ away from its
mode, so this model stresses a different part of the estimator than the other three: the outcome
marginal $\widehat f_Y$ and bandwidth $h_Y$ in the region where data are sparse, rather than the
discriminant's fit near the bulk of the distribution.

\paragraph{Heteroscedastic.} A variance that grows with $t$ through a log link, guaranteeing
positivity, with everything else linear-Gaussian:
\begin{equation}
Y \mid T=t,X=x \ \sim\ \mathcal N\big(a^\top x + b^\top t,\ \sigma_0^2\exp(c^\top t)\big).
\label{eq:app-dgp-heterosc}
\end{equation}
The variance does not depend on $x$, so integrating $x$ against its marginal leaves the model
Gaussian:
\begin{equation}
p_t(y) = \mathcal N\big(y;\, a^\top\mu_X + b^\top t,\ a^\top\Sigma_X a + \sigma_0^2\exp(c^\top t)\big).
\label{eq:app-dgp-heterosc-density}
\end{equation}
$c=0$ recovers the homoscedastic baseline; $\|c\|$ controls how strongly the variance responds to
$t$. The motivation is the one already raised in the introduction of \citet{melnychuk2023flows}:
an estimator built around a single conditional mean and a constant-variance noise model, such as
their TARNet*-based plug-in, cannot represent this dependence at all, by construction; a density
estimator can.

\paragraph{Skewed.} A linear-Gaussian mean with a standard skew-normal noise term, in Azzalini's
stochastic representation: $\delta = \lambda/\sqrt{1+\lambda^2}$,
$\varepsilon = \delta\,|U_0| + \sqrt{1-\delta^2}\,U_1$, $U_0,U_1 \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}
\mathcal N(0,1)$,
\begin{equation}
Y \mid T=t,X=x \;=\; \xi + a^\top x + b^\top t + \omega\,\varepsilon.
\label{eq:app-dgp-skew}
\end{equation}
Unlike the heavy-tailed case, this model has an \emph{exact}, elementary closed form for $p_t(y)$,
from a property specific to this representation of the skew-normal: $\varepsilon$ is a half-normal
term plus an \emph{independent} Gaussian term, and summing an independent Gaussian
($a^\top(X-\mu_X)$, after integrating $X$ to its marginal) with that Gaussian term simply merges
into it, leaving the half-normal part untouched, so $p_t(y)$ is again a skew-normal density,
with an adjusted scale and shape:
\begin{equation}
Y \mid \mathrm{do}(T=t) \ \sim\ \mathrm{SkewNormal}\big(\mathrm{loc} = \xi + a^\top\mu_X + b^\top t,
\ \ \omega' = \sqrt{\omega^2 + \mathrm{var}_X},\ \ \lambda'\big),
\label{eq:app-dgp-skew-density}
\end{equation}
with $\mathrm{var}_X = a^\top\Sigma_X a$, $\delta = \lambda/\sqrt{1+\lambda^2}$,
$\delta' = \delta\,\omega/\omega'$, and $\lambda' = \delta'/\sqrt{1-\delta'^2}$. $\lambda=0$
recovers the symmetric, Gaussian case; $|\lambda|$ large gives strong asymmetry. Unlike the
heteroscedastic and multimodal models, whose parameters move the mean or the variance of
$Y\mid T,X$ with $t$, $\lambda$ leaves both fixed and shifts only the third moment, the same
isolation the heavy-tailed model performs one moment up, for kurtosis rather than skewness.
Sweeping $\lambda$ therefore tests whether a discriminant can pick up an asymmetry that a
location-scale comparison would miss entirely.

\paragraph{A dimension-dependent pitfall.} $\mathrm{var}_X =
a^\top\Sigma_X a$ appears in every closed form above, added to the model's own ``characteristic''
scale ($s_1,s_2$ for the mixture; $\sigma$ for the heavy tail; $\sigma_0$ for the heteroscedastic
model; $\omega$ for the skewed one). When $a$ and $\Sigma_X$ are drawn with entries of fixed scale
as $p=\dim(X)$ grows, $\mathrm{var}_X$ grows roughly linearly in $p$ while the characteristic
scale, fixed by design, does not: at $p$ in the tens, $\mathrm{var}_X$ can already exceed the
characteristic scale by one to two orders of magnitude, at which point the confounding variance
dominates the sum and the very property each model was built to exhibit, bimodality, heavy
tails, heteroscedasticity, skewness, becomes numerically negligible in $p_t(y)$, not because the
outcome mechanism changed, but because it was diluted by an unrelated, dimension-dependent term.
This effect (verified numerically, not assumed) is controlled for throughout
Section~\ref{sec:sim-experiments} by rescaling each model's characteristic scale in proportion to
$\sqrt{\mathrm{var}_X}$ at every value of $p=\dim(X)$ visited in the dimension sweeps, so that the
targeted property remains at a comparable, detectable strength across the whole sweep rather than
only at the smallest dimension.

\subsection{Real and semi-synthetic datasets}
\label{app:dgp-real}

Four further datasets appear in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, but not in this paper's own
simulation study; they are documented here for completeness, since none of them
is otherwise defined in the main text. All four have a binary treatment, real or semi-synthetic
covariates, and simulated (hence known) potential outcomes, which is what makes them usable for
interventional density estimation benchmarking despite being real-data-based. IHDP \citep{hill2011ihdp} pairs $25$ real
covariates from a randomized early-intervention study for low-birth-weight infants
($n\approx 747$) with two simulated response surfaces; it has a small, fixed, realistic
$n$ and $\dim(X)$, the opposite of AXY's covariate-dimension sweep above, where every method has
enough data to be estimated reasonably well and the comparison is not dominated by a
dimensionality effect. ACIC 2016 \citep{dorie2019acic2016} and
ACIC 2018 \citep{shimoni2018acic2018} are collections of, respectively, $77$ and $24$
semi-synthetic datasets built from real covariates (linked birth and infant death records, for
2018) under a range of simulated outcome-generating mechanisms, designed as a data-analysis
competition benchmark rather than a single fixed design. HC-MNIST \citep{jesson2021hcmnist}
replaces tabular covariates by $28\times 28$ MNIST images \citep{lecun1998mnist}, augmented with
one further hidden (unobserved) confounder, so that the covariate is high-dimensional and
positivity is deliberately violated by construction.

\section{Cross-validation and hyperparameter grids}
\label{app:cv}

Every method has hyperparameters, the penalty $\lambda$, the Monte Carlo size $S_{\mathrm{mc}}$, and, for the
neural-network discriminant, the parameters governing the architecture
itself, and all of them are selected once per configuration by a $5$-fold
cross-validation on the training portion of that configuration, then held fixed across the $50$
replications of Section~\ref{sec:sim-experiments}, exactly as \citet{melnychuk2023flows} tune
their own baselines once, on the first of their ten folds, and freeze the result for the rest.

\paragraph{Tuning criterion.} It deliberately never touches the causal target, but not, in the
end, through the contrast classifier's own binary cross-entropy as first proposed: computing a
held-out cross-entropy for $D_{\betahat_\lambda}$ would need a public scoring hook on the
discriminant that the implementation does not expose. What is used instead
reuses only $\widehat p^{\,\mathrm{ifc}}_t(y)$ itself, already available from \eqref{eq:estimator}:
the held-out \emph{factual} log-likelihood,
\begin{equation}
\log\text{-lik}(\text{held-out fold}) \;=\; \frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{val}}}\sum_{i \in \mathrm{val}}
\log \widehat p_{T_i}(Y_i),
\label{eq:cv-loglik}
\end{equation}
each term evaluated at the observation's own factual $(T_i,Y_i)$, never at a counterfactual value.
Because $\widehat p_t(y)$ is marginal in $t$, it does not condition on $X$ by construction;
\eqref{eq:cv-loglik} ignores $X_i$ entirely, an intentional weakening relative to a
criterion that could condition on it (as the flow/TARNet baselines' own nuisance tuning does);
what it keeps is the property that matters here, shared with the cross-entropy originally
proposed and with \citet{melnychuk2023flows}'s own nuisance tuning: only observed, factual
quantities are used, so tuning is a statement about how well a nuisance is estimated, never about
how close the resulting causal estimate happens to land to a truth that would not be available
outside a simulation. On the four data-generating processes of Appendix~\ref{app:dgp-continuous},
where the true $p_t(y)$ \emph{is} available, \eqref{eq:cv-loglik} and causal accuracy are compared
directly as a check that the two are not systematically at odds, but the former, not the latter,
is what selects the hyperparameters actually used.

\paragraph{Grids.} A single grid across every configuration would under-serve both ends of the
dimension range this paper targets: a $\lambda$ or a discriminant capacity tuned for
$\dim(X)+\dim(T)$ in the tens has no reason to remain adequate in the thousands. Table~\ref{tab:cv-grids}
therefore gives three grids, indexed by $\dim(X)+\dim(T)$ of the configuration being tuned, each
shifting its ranges rather than merely repeating the smaller one at a larger scale: $\lambda$'s
upper end grows with dimension, following directly from the diagnosis in
Remark~\ref{rem:impl-logistic} that too small a $\lambda$ is specifically dangerous under a
misspecified $\phi$; the neural-network discriminant's capacity (hidden width) grows to give it
a chance of using the extra dimensions rather than being capacity-starved by a grid sized for a
much smaller problem.

\section{Glossary of interventional density estimators}
\label{app:glossary}

Section~\ref{sec:intro}: the abbreviation used for it throughout, what it does, which of the
three families of Section~\ref{sec:intro} it belongs to, whether it corrects the first-order
plug-in bias of Section~\ref{sec:setting-plugin} or not, and its source. All nine assume a
treatment with finitely many levels and estimate the conditional outcome density directly (or,
for \textsc{dkme}, a kernel embedding of it), which is precisely the high-dimensional,
back-door object Sections~\ref{sec:setting}--\ref{sec:eif} of this paper build a continuous,
multidimensional-treatment alternative to.

The three families group the estimators by what they represent and how: the flow-based family
represents the outcome law directly as one or two normalizing flows, and is the most flexible
but also the most fully parametric, so every member can misspecify the flow's functional form;
the regression-plugin family fits a shared neural representation and reads off a fixed output
family (Gaussian, or a Gaussian mixture) from it, cheaper but restricted to that family, and
uncorrected, so it inherits the first-order bias of Section~\ref{sec:setting-plugin} exactly;
and the kernel/embedding family is nonparametric in the outcome, at the price of the same
curse-of-dimensionality in the conditioning set $(T,X)$ that motivates
Section~\ref{sec:mcd-correction}'s use of marginal contrastive discrimination in the first
place. \textsc{mcd-pi} and \textsc{mcd-ifc}, last in the table, are a fourth family of one:
neither a flow, a fixed-family regression, nor a kernel estimator of the conditional density,
but a reduction to a univariate density and a binary classifier, uncorrected in the first case
and one-step-corrected, through its own factorization rather than through $\gps$, in the
second.
